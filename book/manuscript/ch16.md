---
title: Manifestation Is a Divine Word
lede: The word the manifest space borrowed belongs to God. Reclaiming it is not a rhetorical trick; it is the whole argument in miniature.
---

Begin with the word, because the word is where the battle is.

*Manifest* comes into English from the Latin *manifestus* — "caught in the hand," hence palpable, evident, plain. It is the opposite of hidden. In the New Testament it renders a family of Greek words: *phaneroō*, to make visible or known; *phanerōsis*, a making-visible; *epiphaneia*, an appearing, from which we get *Epiphany*. None of these words, in any of their occurrences, means what the manifest space means. Not one of them describes a human being causing an event to appear by the power of thought.

They describe something else, and the something else is the subject of this chapter.

::scene

**Manifestation is what God does.**

"He was manifested in the flesh" (1 Tim 3:16). The sentence is a hymn fragment, quoted by Paul as something already familiar, and it is one of the densest Christological statements in the New Testament. What is being said is not that a divine idea became vivid. It is that the eternal, invisible, unimaginable God entered a particular human life at a particular time in a particular place, and could be seen, heard, and touched.

John says the same thing with the authority of a witness: "That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life — the life was made manifest, and we saw it" (1 John 1:1–2). The accumulation of senses is deliberate. He is insisting on the physicality, because the physicality is the point.

And the prologue of the fourth Gospel states the principle that governs everything else: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). The manifestation of God is not a thought that becomes clearer. It is a person who becomes present.

Now notice the shape of this. Something hidden becomes visible. Something interior becomes external. Something eternal enters time. Something invisible takes on flesh. A word becomes a body.

That is *exactly* the shape of the manifest space's grammar — hidden becomes visible, interior becomes external, word becomes fact — and it is the exact inversion of it in every respect that matters. In the Gospel, the one who manifests is God, the means is a person, the result is a gift, and the effect on the recipient is worship. In the manifest space, the one who manifests is the self, the means is a technique, the result is a possession, and the effect on the recipient is satisfaction.

The same shape, turned inside out. That is what a parody is.

::scene

**Manifestation is what God does to the believer.**

"To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (1 Cor 12:7). The Greek is *phanerōsis tou pneumatos* — the making-visible of the Spirit — and the sentence is a bombshell, because of the last three words.

The manifestation is given, not achieved. It is given to each, not earned by the exceptional. Its content is the Spirit, not a result. And its purpose is the common good, not the private benefit of the recipient.

Every one of those four points is a direct contradiction of the manifest space, and they arrive in a single clause of a letter written to a quarrelsome community in a port city. The Corinthians were, among other things, a congregation of spiritual overachievers — proud of their gifts, ranking them, using them for status — and Paul's response was not to deny the gifts but to reorient them. The manifestation is real. It is spectacular. And it is not yours.

This is the chapter of Scripture that should be printed on the wall of every Catholic retreat centre, because it settles the whole question of what a manifestation is for. If the Spirit is manifested *for the common good*, then a practice whose entire structure is private benefit has not merely got the metaphysics wrong. It has got the direction of the gift wrong.

::scene

**Manifestation is what creation is waiting for.**

"For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God" (Rom 8:19). The Greek is *apokalypsis* — an unveiling — and the passage it belongs to is the most important passage in the New Testament for this book.

Paul has been describing a world in bondage: creation subjected to futility, groaning in travail, waiting. And what it waits for is not a delivery. It is a *revelation* — the appearance, in glory, of what the children of God actually are. "It does not yet appear what we shall be, but we know that when he appears we shall be like him" (1 John 3:2).

Put these together and something remarkable emerges. The manifest space's central desire — that the invisible become visible, that the interior become external, that what is hidden in me be made real in the world — is *not a false desire*. It is the desire of creation. It is the groaning of Romans 8. It is precisely what the Gospel promises.

What the manifest space has done is take the deepest desire of the human person and shrink it to the size of a personal outcome. The thing we are waiting for is not the job. It is the revelation of who we are in Christ, and the creation is on tiptoe for it.

::scene

**Manifestation is presently hidden.**

"Your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ who is your life is manifested, then you also will be manifested with him in glory" (Col 3:1–4).

This is the sentence that makes the whole thing bearable, and it is the sentence the manifest space cannot survive.

The Christian's manifestation is *deferred*. It is real — as real as the risen body, as real as the glory that will be revealed — but it is not now, and the not-now is not a failure. It is the structure of the life of faith. The Christian is hidden: in Christ, in the Church, in the ordinary, in the sacraments, in a life that often looks like nothing at all. And the hiddenness is not a problem to be solved by technique. It is a place to live.

The manifest space cannot say this, because the manifest space exists to eliminate the gap. Its whole premise is that the delay is a malfunction — resistance, misalignment, low vibration — and that the technique closes it. But the delay is not a malfunction. It is the space in which faith, hope, and love become possible. If everything were manifested now, there would be nothing to hope for, and hope is the one thing that makes a human life livable.

::scene

Now the reclamation, stated as a proposal.

**Stop conceding the word.** Every time a Catholic writer uses "manifestation" in quotation marks, as though it were a term belonging to the phenomenon under critique, the Church loses a little ground. The word is ours. It has been ours for two thousand years. The phenomenon borrowed it, as the phenomenon has borrowed everything else — the sense of providence, the power of words, the reality of the interior life, the practice of gratitude, the hope of transformation — and it borrowed them because it had nothing of its own.

**Teach the direction.** The single most useful catechetical sentence in this whole area is: *manifestation is something God does.* Everything follows from that. If God manifests, then the appropriate posture is receptivity, and the appropriate practices are prayer, sacrament, and obedience. If the self manifests, then the appropriate posture is technique, and the appropriate practices are methods. Two spiritualities, two postures, two kinds of person.

**Recover the feast.** The Church already has a liturgy of manifestation: Epiphany, the showing-forth of Christ to the nations; the Presentation, the meeting in the temple; the Transfiguration, the glory briefly visible; the Resurrection appearances; the Ascension; Pentecost. These are not decorations on a doctrinal system. They are the Church's annual school of manifestation, and a person who has prayed them for twenty years knows more about the subject than any coach.

**Name the desire correctly.** When someone says, "I want to manifest a better life," the accurate translation is usually: *I want to become the person I am supposed to be, and I want it to show.* That is not a bad desire. It is Romans 8 in colloquial English. The pastoral task is not to suppress it but to name it, and then to say where it is actually fulfilled.

::scene

There is a risk in this proposal, and it should be named.

If you tell people "manifestation is a Catholic word," some of them will hear "so my practice is fine." That is why this chapter cannot stand alone. The reclamation of the word only works together with the audit — with the fourteen verdicts, the four tests, the lexicon, the refusals. The word is ours *because* the thing the manifest space does with it is a parody, and a parody is only corrected by the original, not by the abandonment of the term.

The two halves of this book belong together. Part Two without Part Three is a demolition. Part Three without Part Two is a surrender with better vocabulary. What is wanted is the thing that is hardest to hold: the word, the doctrine, and the refusal, in one hand.
