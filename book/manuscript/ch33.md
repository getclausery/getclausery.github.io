title: Listening
lede: The first institutional act is not a refutation but a description. Here is how to do the Moerbeke work in 2026 — who to talk to, what to ask, and the disciplines that keep it honest.
---

Thomas's first move was to commission translations. He did not begin with a verdict; he began with the text. The modern equivalent is not a translation of Greek into Latin but an act of attention: sitting with the people who practise manifestation until you can describe what they do in terms they would accept.

This chapter is a plan for that. It is deliberately concrete, because the difference between a description and a polemic is usually a matter of method, and method can be written down.

::scene

### Why this is not optional

Three reasons, in ascending order of importance.

**Because you will get the facts wrong otherwise.** The manifest space is not one thing. It has at least four live variants (theistic, mentalist, energetic, psychologized), a dozen media formats, and a vocabulary that shifts every eighteen months. A person who has read *The Secret* knows one variant, from 2006, and will be speaking to people who have never heard of it.

**Because contempt is epistemically fatal.** The moment you decide the person in front of you is a fool, you stop being able to learn from her, and she stops telling you the truth. This is not a counsel of niceness; it is a condition of accurate knowledge.

**Because the description is itself a form of respect, and respect is the first thing the Church owes.** The person who has been manifesting for six years has been trying to make her life bearable. She has been doing something wrong. She has not been doing nothing. A description that can hold both of those facts is the only kind that can be used.

::scene

### Who to talk to

Not a random sample; a strategic one. Six groups, roughly ten to fifteen people each.

1. **Practitioners who are thriving.** Not the ones in crisis. The ones for whom it is working, who have a practice and a community and a vocabulary. They will tell you what the system looks like from the inside when it is at its best, which is the version you have to answer.
2. **Practitioners who are frightened.** The ones who have begun to suspect that it is harming them, or who are scrupulous about their thoughts, or who have been told that their illness is their fault. They will tell you the cost.
3. **Defectors.** People who left. They will tell you what broke, and — this is the valuable part — what they miss.
4. **Catholic hybridizers.** People who do both. They are the largest group and the least studied, and they are the actual pastoral situation. They are not confused; they have a working arrangement, and the arrangement is what you have to understand.
5. **Clergy and catechists.** What they are asked, what they answer, what they avoid.
6. **The young.** Under twenty-five, because the vocabulary is theirs and it is already mutating away from the books.

::scene

### What to ask

Six questions, in this order. Do not ask about beliefs first; ask about practice. Beliefs are reconstructed after the fact, and people are unreliable about them.

1. **"Walk me through what you did yesterday."** Concrete. Time, place, object, words.
2. **"When did you start, and what was happening in your life?"** The origin is usually a crisis.
3. **"What has it done for you?"** Let them tell you the good, at length, without qualifying it.
4. **"What has it cost you?"** Ask this later, not earlier, and only after the first three.
5. **"What would you do if it stopped working?"** The diagnostic from chapter 32, applied in research.
6. **"Who knows about it?"** The diagnostic from chapter 21.

And one question for the Catholics: **"How do you hold the two together?"** The answer will be some version of compartmentalization, and the shape of the compartment is the real theological problem.

::scene

### The disciplines

Four rules for the listener, and each of them is a guard against a specific failure.

**State the position in its strongest form before criticizing it.** Write the steel man down. Read it to a practitioner and ask: "Is that fair?" If she says no, rewrite it. Do not proceed to the critique until she says yes.

**Do not correct during the interview.** Not once. Note the error, and keep listening. The correction can come in the book, in the sermon, in the conversation six months later. If you correct in the interview, you will get compliance and lose the data.

**Record the good.** The temptation is to collect harms, because harms make the case. But a description that contains only harms is not a description; it is an indictment, and it will be recognized as one by the people it is about.

**Say what you do not know.** In writing. The manifest space is full of confident claims; the answer to a confident claim is not another confident claim. It is a description with its limits marked, which is what the best scholarship does and what most polemic does not.

::scene

### The six-month plan

**Months 1–2: reading.** The primary texts, not the secondary. *The Secret*; Neville's *Feeling Is the Secret* and *The Power of Awareness*; a Hicks book, for the metaphysics; one contemporary popularizer (Nafousi or equivalent) for the current idiom; two or three of the Catholic-adjacent titles, because they are the actual competition. Plus the 2003 Pontifical Council document, which is the best official description available in any language.

**Months 2–4: interviews.** Sixty to eighty conversations, in the six groups above. A written protocol, so the data are comparable. An ethics of consent: no names, no identifying details, no recording without permission, and an agreement to show each person the paragraph in which she appears.

**Months 3–5: the content audit.** A coded sample of the top few hundred items of manifestation media by reach: what claims are made, in what order, with what proof, with what call to action. Code by the seven-point grammar from chapter 2, and note where the grammar has mutated.

**Month 6: the description.** A hundred pages, written in ordinary prose, with the practitioner's own words in it, and a final chapter on what the Church has that the manifest space lacks. Circulate it to critics on both sides before publication. Publish it even if it is unflattering to your own position.

::scene

### What the description will probably find

Three predictions, offered so that they can be falsified.

**First, that the manifest space is much less metaphysical than its critics think.** Most practitioners do not hold a cosmology. They hold a practice, a hope, and a phrase. The cosmology lives in the books and the coaches, and it is absorbed by a minority.

**Second, that the harms are concentrated in two places:** the sick and the scrupulous. The healthy practitioner with a journal and a job is, in most cases, doing something harmless that could be baptized in an afternoon. The person who has been told that her cancer is her fault is in a different situation entirely, and the difference is not one of degree.

**Third, that the Catholic hybridizers are the largest and most neglected group, and that they are not confused.** They have made a working peace between two systems, and the peace is unstable, and they know it. They are waiting for someone to explain the difference, and the explanation they have received so far has been either a prohibition or a shrug.

If those three predictions hold, the strategy of this book holds with them: describe, distinguish, refuse, replace, and institutionalize. If they fail — if, say, the cosmology turns out to be widely and explicitly held — then the audit needs to move earlier in the process and the catechesis needs to be more basic than this book assumes.

Which is why the listening comes first. A plan that cannot be corrected by the data is not a plan; it is a position.
