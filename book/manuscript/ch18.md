---
title: The Groaning Creation
lede: Romans 8 gives the manifest space's desire a cosmic scope and a different object. The world is not a mirror; it is a fellow creature, in labour, waiting with us.
---

There is a passage in Romans 8 that reads, if you come to it from the manifest space, like a message in a bottle from a better version of the same longing.

Paul has been arguing that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed. Then he does something unexpected: he puts the *world* into the sentence. "For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God… we know that the whole creation has been groaning in travail together until now; and not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Rom 8:19, 22–23).

The manifest space has a cosmology. It is a projection theory: the world is an outpicturing of consciousness, a mirror, a screen. Romans 8 has a cosmology too, and it is not that. The world is a *creature*: made, good, wounded, subjected to futility, groaning, waiting. And it is waiting for the same thing we are — the manifestation of the children of God.

::scene

Two cosmologies, and the difference between them is not academic.

In the manifest space, the world has no independent standing. It is a reflection, and a reflection has no rights. You cannot owe anything to a mirror. This is why the manifest space, for all its talk of energy and connection, has no real doctrine of creation, and therefore no real doctrine of the environment, and no real doctrine of the body, and no real doctrine of the neighbour. A world that is a projection can be used without limit, because there is nothing there to use.

In Romans 8, the world is a fellow creature. It has been subjected to futility — note that Paul does not say by whom; the passive is careful — and it groans, and it waits, and its fate is bound up with ours. "The creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God."

This is one of the strongest possible grounds for a Christian ecology, and it is also the strongest possible ground for saying that the manifest space's relationship to the material world is not "too material" but "not material enough." A person who believes the world is a mirror will treat it as a surface. A person who believes the world is a creature will treat it as a companion.

::scene

There is a second thing in Romans 8, and it is the answer to a question the manifest space raises and cannot answer.

The manifest space has a great deal to say about the present and almost nothing to say about the future. Its eschatology is realized: the thing is now, or the technique is wrong. Romans 8 is the opposite. It says that the whole creation — and we with it — is in labour, and that the labour is not a malfunction but a birth.

The image of childbirth is exact and it does no sentimentalizing. Labour is real pain, it is not chosen in the moment, it is not a punishment, and it ends. The one giving birth does not "manifest" the child by aligning her frequency; she undergoes something that is happening to her and through her, and the thing that comes out is not a projection of her consciousness but a person.

This is the structure of Christian hope, and it is what makes the manifest space's optimism look like what it is: a refusal to wait. Optimism says the pain is not real or is about to end. Hope says the pain is real, the birth is real, and the two are connected, and the connection is not visible yet.

::scene

Now the warning, because Romans 8 is also the chapter that the manifest space's cousin, the "cosmic Christ" movement, uses for its own purposes. The 2003 Pontifical Council document flagged this precisely: in New Age thought, "Christ" is a title for a state of consciousness, and Jesus of Nazareth was one of many who attained it (§2.3.4.2; §3.3). The cosmic Christ becomes a principle of universal energy, and the historical Jesus evaporates into an example.

The Catholic doctrine of the cosmic Christ is entirely different and much more concrete. "He is the image of the invisible God, the first-born of all creation; for in him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible… all things were created through him and for him. He is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (Col 1:15–17). And then, the sentence that makes it Christian: "For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell, and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:19–20).

Notice the difference. The New Age cosmic Christ is a *principle* that explains the universe. The Catholic cosmic Christ is a *person* through whom the universe was made and is being reconciled, and the reconciliation happens "by the blood of his cross" — which is to say, through a particular death at a particular time, under a particular empire. The principle is available to anyone with the right consciousness. The person is available to anyone who will receive him.

The manifest space's "universe" is a degraded version of this doctrine. It has the universality and the responsiveness and the sense that everything is connected — all of which are true — and it has lost the person, the cross, and the creation. What is left is a cosmos that feels like God and has none of the properties of God: no will, no face, no history, no wounds.

::scene

Which brings us to the practical point of this chapter, and it is unexpectedly ordinary.

If the world is a creature — if it groans, if it waits, if it is a fellow and not a screen — then the way you treat the material world is a spiritual matter in a much stronger sense than "stewardship."

The food you eat is a creature. The body you inhabit is a creature, and it is not a projection of your beliefs about it; it is a gift with its own integrity, its own limits, its own mortality. The person who cleans your office is a creature. The weather is a creature. None of them is a mirror, and none of them exists to tell you about your vibration.

This is why the Church's materiality — water, oil, bread, wine, incense, stone, stained glass, fasting, feasting, pilgrimage, relics, bodies laid in the ground — is not a lower stage of religion from which a mature person graduates into pure spirituality. It is the religion of the Incarnation, and the Incarnation is the doctrine that matter can carry God.

The manifest space's ideal is a world you can influence without touching. The Catholic ideal is a world you touch, and that touches you back, and in which God is present in a piece of bread.

That is not a smaller vision. It is the only vision in which the groaning of Romans 8 makes sense, because it is the only vision in which the world is real enough to groan.
