---
title: Paris, 1277
lede: What actually happened when Aristotle arrived, why the first answer was a ban, and why the ban failed.
---

The story usually told is too tidy. In the tidy version, Aristotle's works were rediscovered, Thomas Aquinas read them, saw that they were compatible with the faith, and wrote the *Summa*. The Church was pleased, and philosophy has been Catholic ever since.

The actual story is messier, longer, and far more useful. It involves three rounds of bans, a faculty strike, a vendetta between two bishops, a Dominican order defending its controversial brother, a condemnation that hit him after his death, a canonization that vindicated him fifty years later, and a revival six hundred years after that. It is, in other words, the normal shape of a real intellectual integration: not a seminar but a fight.

::scene

Aristotle's works came west in waves. The *Categories* and *De Interpretatione* had been known through Boethius; the rest — the *Physics*, the *Metaphysics*, *De Anima*, the *Ethics*, the *Politics* — arrived in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, translated from Arabic, often by way of Hebrew, in Toledo and Sicily, and accompanied by Islamic commentaries of extraordinary sophistication: al-Farabi, Avicenna, and above all Averroes, whose reading of Aristotle was so powerful that Latin scholars called him simply "the Commentator."

By 1250 the Latin West possessed a complete, coherent, and philosophically superior account of nature, causation, the soul, and human happiness, written by a pagan who had never heard of Moses. The implications were not subtle. Aristotle's cosmos was eternal, not created. His God was a self-thinking thought that did not know the world, let alone love it. His soul was the form of the body and, on the most rigorous reading, did not survive it. His ethics delivered flourishing without grace, and his politics delivered the good life without the Church.

The first institutional response was, predictably, prohibition. The statutes of the University of Paris forbade the teaching of Aristotle's natural philosophy in 1210 and 1215; a papal commission in 1231 ordered that the offending books be "purged of errors" before use. The prohibition did not hold, because the books were too useful and too interesting. By 1255 the arts faculty had made them required texts. You cannot ban a better tool.

::scene

The crisis that followed was not really about Aristotle. It was about **autonomy** — whether there is a domain of truth that philosophy can settle on its own, with conclusions that theology must then accommodate.

The figure who pressed this hardest was Siger of Brabant, a master of arts in Paris, whose positions were read as teaching that what is true in philosophy may be false in faith, and vice versa. The "double truth" formula is probably a caricature — Siger's actual position was more like a professional restriction: the philosopher reports what reason concludes, and leaves the faith aside. But the practical effect was the same. It created a class of Christian intellectuals who taught, six days a week, a cosmology that contradicted the Creed, and who regarded this as a matter of academic method.

Three responses formed, and it is worth naming them, because they recur in every crisis of this kind, including ours.

**The first was the traditionalist refusal.** Bonaventure, the Franciscan general, preaching in Paris in 1273, warned against the "waters of Aristotle" and treated philosophical autonomy as a spiritual danger. His instinct was not obscurantist; it was pastoral. He had watched the arts faculty turn the study of nature into a substitute for the study of God.

**The second was compartmentalization.** The arts masters who taught Aristotle's cosmos while professing the Creed, on the understanding that the two belonged to different registers and need not be reconciled. This is the position of every Catholic who says "I'm Catholic on Sunday and I manifest on Monday; they're different things."

**The third was transformative integration.** Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican, a fat and slow-moving man who wrote at dictation speed and was, by all accounts, socially awkward, spent his life on the problem. He did not begin with a verdict. He began by commissioning translations.

::scene

That detail is worth pausing on, because it is the least romantic and most revealing fact about the whole enterprise. Thomas did not read Aristotle in snippets from a florilegium. He asked his friend William of Moerbeke to produce literal, word-for-word Latin translations of the Greek, and he worked from those. He wrote commentaries on the *Physics*, the *Metaphysics*, *De Anima*, the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the *Posterior Analytics*, and the *De Caelo*. He wrote a *Summa contra Gentiles* that is, among other things, a manual for arguing with philosophers on their own ground. Only then did he use Aristotle in theology.

The sequence matters. **Description preceded judgment by about fifteen years.** Every serious failure in Catholic engagement with the manifest space that I have seen — and I have seen many — fails at this exact point. The critic has read the summary, not the source; the verdict has preceded the description; and the person in front of him, who has actually done the practice, can tell.

::scene

Thomas's integration had a structure, and the structure is what this book borrows. Part Two sets out the method in detail; here it is enough to see the shape.

He took Aristotle's **instruments**: the logic, the four causes, the analysis of change in terms of act and potency, the psychology of habit and virtue, the account of the passions, the distinction between the speculative and practical intellect. These were tools, and tools do not have a religion.

He refused Aristotle's **framework**: the eternity of the world, the necessity of all things, the denial of providence, the mortality of the soul, the self-enclosed God who does not know particulars. He refused them not as "pagan errors" but as *philosophical* errors, and he argued against them philosophically, often using Aristotle's own distinctions.

He subordinated the whole to **revealed principles**. This is the meaning of *sacra doctrina*: theology is a science whose principles are given, and philosophy is its instrument. The instrument is not despised — a good instrument is precious — but it does not set the questions.

And he accepted that this would cost him. It did.

::scene

On 7 March 1277, Étienne Tempier, Bishop of Paris, issued a condemnation of 219 propositions. The immediate occasion was a request from Pope John XXI, who was worried about divination and determinism; the deeper occasion was a long-running conflict between the bishop and the arts faculty, and between the secular clergy and the mendicant orders. The propositions condemned included a great deal that Thomas had never taught, a good deal that he had, and several that were the common property of the schools.

The most famous is the claim that God cannot make a vacuum, or that he cannot move the heavens in a straight line, or that he cannot create several worlds — the "absolute power" questions. The condemnation was aimed at protecting divine freedom from Aristotelian necessity. In the process it also hit propositions about the unity of substantial form and about the intellect, which were close to Thomas's own positions.

Thomas had died in 1274, on his way to the Council of Lyon. He could not answer. The Dominican order did: the provincial chapters defended his teaching, and the order made his doctrine its own. He was canonized in 1323. In 1325, the Bishop of Paris's successor revoked the condemnation of the propositions that were understood to be his. By the sixteenth century his *Summa* was the standard textbook of Catholic theology. In 1879, *Aeterni Patris* made his doctrine the norm of Catholic philosophical formation. In 1998, *Fides et Ratio* called him "an authentic model for all who seek the truth," while insisting that the Church "has no philosophy of her own nor does she canonize any one particular philosophy in preference to others" (§§44, 49).

Two things about this timeline should be pressed into the reader's mind.

**First, the condemnation was not simply a mistake and not simply a persecution.** It was the institutional immune system doing what immune systems do: responding to a real threat (necessitarian philosophy claiming autonomy) with a blunt instrument (a list of forbidden sentences) that damaged some healthy tissue (Thomas's own positions) and missed some of the disease (the condemned propositions continued to be taught, in subtler forms, for centuries). The Church's magisterium is not a truth-machine. It is a living authority that sometimes acts badly and sometimes corrects itself, and both facts belong in the account.

**Second, the integration took roughly fifty years from Thomas's first commentary to the general acceptance of his doctrine, and six hundred to the point where it became a monument — at which point it began to function as a constraint rather than a resource, which is why John Paul II had to say what he said in 1998.**

::scene

Now the translation.

The manifest space is not Aristotle. It is not a science, it is not written in a language that requires a Moerbeke, and it does not have a Siger of Brabant. But the *structure* of the crisis is identical, and the three responses are already forming.

The traditionalists are already here: the priests and writers who say it is all New Age, all dangerous, all the same thing, and who are sometimes right about specific practices and always wrong about the people. They will produce a ban, or the local equivalent of one, and the ban will fail for the same reason the 1210 statutes failed: the thing is too useful and too interesting to the people who need it, and a prohibition does not supply an alternative.

The compartmentalizers are already here, and they are the majority. They are the Catholics who pray the Rosary and script their manifestations and see no contradiction, because they have been formed by a culture in which the two occupy different rooms and never meet. They are not hypocrites. They are the direct product of a catechesis that taught them doctrine in one register and left practice to the market.

The integrators are barely here. There are a few books, a handful of podcasts, some well-meaning syntheses that keep the metaphysics and add holy water. Nothing yet that has done the Moerbeke work — that has read the sources closely enough to describe them without contempt, and then rebuilt the practice inside a different causality.

Which means the field is open, and the timetable is long, and the first thing to do is not to write a refutation. It is to do what Thomas did in the fifteen years before he wrote a single theological sentence about Aristotle: to read the thing, in its own language, until you can say what it says better than its own defenders can.

That is what the next part of this book attempts. It will be uncomfortable for readers on both sides, which is the ordinary sign that the work is being done honestly.
