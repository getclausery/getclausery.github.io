---
title: Institutions, or Why a Sermon Needs a Building
lede: Ideas do not survive on their merits. They survive in institutions — and the manifest space has better ones than we do. Seven institutions the Church would need to build, and the governance that keeps them honest.
---

Thomas's *Summa* survived because of the Dominican order, the University of Paris, and a century of teachers who used it. The idea was excellent; the institution is what made it last.

This is the least romantic chapter in the book and possibly the most important. Everything in Parts One to Four can be true and still evaporate, because truth does not reproduce itself. It is transmitted by people, in institutions, over generations, and the manifest space understands this better than the Church currently does. It has publishers, platforms, coaching certifications, conferences, apps, and — above all — a delivery system that puts a thirty-second practice in front of a lonely person at eleven at night.

The Church has parishes. They are, potentially, the most powerful delivery system in the world. At present, in most places, they are not being used for this.

::scene

### 1. A centre

Not a bureaucracy: a small research centre, attached to a university or a diocese, with three or four people and a library. Its job is to do the listening (chapter 33), maintain the description, and answer enquiries from bishops, seminary rectors, and journalists.

The model is the small institute rather than the large faculty: a place where two or three people who know the material can be found by the people who need it. The cost is the cost of three salaries and a room. The alternative is that every diocese improvises, badly, and that the same questions are answered from scratch two hundred times a year.

### 2. A journal, or a standing section

The scholarly infrastructure matters less for scholars than for legitimacy. A body of work with peer review, footnotes, and a place to publish corrections is a body of work that can be argued with. A body of work that exists only in podcasts cannot be corrected, and will drift.

If a new journal is not feasible, a standing section in an existing one — *Nova et Vetera*, *Communio*, *The Thomist*, a diocesan theological review — would do. The point is not the masthead. The point is a place where a claim can be tested in public and where a correction is possible.

### 3. A formation module

Two audiences, two products.

**For seminarians and clergy:** a single-semester module, taught from the disputed questions, covering the phenomenon, the four tests, the triage, and the practice. The outcome is not expertise; it is the capacity to answer the question in the car park without doing harm. Every seminary could have this within two years if someone wrote the syllabus.

**For laypeople:** a short course — six sessions — for parishes, campus ministries, and parents. The outcome is the same, at a lower level, plus the practices from Part Four. This is the product that scales, and it should be free.

### 4. A publisher, and a free library

The manifest space is free at the point of use, and the response must be too. This is not a funding strategy; it is a credibility strategy. A person who has been paying a coach will not trust a free pamphlet less for being free; she will trust it more.

The model: a small imprint for books, and a digital library — the disputed questions, the lexicon, the practices, the triage card, the homilies — under an open licence, translated, printable, and usable offline. The whole of this book is a proof of concept: it can be built in a repository and given away.

### 5. A community

This is the hardest and the most necessary. Institutions outlast programmes; charisms outlast institutions. If this work is to survive fifty years, it needs people whose *vocation* it is — a religious community, a secular institute, or an ecclesial movement with this charism at its heart.

The precedent is the Divine Mercy devotion, which survived a nineteen-year suppression because the Marians kept it alive, and the liturgical movement, which was carried by abbeys. A body of writing without a body of people is a library with no readers.

It is also the hardest to plan. One does not found a charism; one can only make room for one, and wait. But a bishop or a superior can do the making-room, and that is the institutional act.

### 6. A network of directors

The single most effective pastoral intervention described in this book is a person with a spiritual director, and the single greatest bottleneck is that there are not enough directors.

What is needed is not a certification industry — which would replicate the manifest space's coaching market — but a network: formation for lay directors, a register that a diocese can publish, a rule of free service, and a supervision structure. The model is the Church's own tradition of *anamchara* and of the lay retreat director, not the therapeutic marketplace.

### 7. A diocesan desk

Every diocese has a marriage tribunal, a liturgical office, and a catechetical office. Almost none has anyone whose job is to answer questions about the New Age, the occult, and popular spirituality — which are, by volume, among the most common questions the faithful actually ask.

A single person, half-time, with a phone number and a page on the diocesan website, would do more than a conference. Their tools are the triage card, the norms of 1978 and 2024, a list of exorcists and clinicians to refer to, and the humility to say "I don't know; let me find out."

### The governance that keeps it honest

Five rules, derived from the failures catalogued in chapter 7 and the norms of the Church.

**Submit to a bishop, from the beginning.** Not at the end, when the work is complete and the ask is a blessing. From the beginning, when the work can still be shaped. The norms put the local Ordinary first for a reason: the process is not a formality, and a project that cannot be supervised cannot be trusted.

**Appoint a censor of books before you need one.** Have every text read by someone with the authority and the independence to stop it. Publish the fact that you do.

**Never sell spiritual power.** Courses may be free or at cost; books may be sold; nothing that promises a spiritual effect may be priced. The negative criterion in the Church's own norms — "evidence of a search for profit or gain" — is not a suggestion.

**Publish your failures.** Every pilot that did not work, every practice that did not land, every prediction from chapter 33 that turned out false. The manifest space's epistemology is unfalsifiable; the response must be conspicuously falsifiable, and the only way to demonstrate that is to be seen being corrected.

**Do not build a personality.** The single greatest risk to a project like this is that it becomes a platform. The form should be designed so that the work can continue without any particular person — which means multiple authors, open materials, and a refusal to put a face on the front of it.

::scene

### What it would cost

A centre, a journal, a course, a library, a network, and a desk: in the first five years, the cost of perhaps a dozen people and a modest budget for translation and printing. That is less than a single mid-sized parish plant.

The return, if it works, is not measurable in the ordinary way. It is the difference between a generation that has a practice of prayer and a generation that has a practice.

::scene

The manifest space did not win because it was true. It won because it was *available*: on the phone, at night, for free, in a form a lonely person could follow. The Church's answer will not be a better argument. It will be a better availability — a place to go, a person to ask, a practice to do, a book that is free, and a community that will not charge for hope.

That is an institutional problem, and it has institutional solutions. The good news is that the institution already exists. The bad news is that it is not, at present, pointed at this.

Pointing it is the work of a generation. The next chapter is about how we would know whether it was working.
