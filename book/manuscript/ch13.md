---
title: Fourteen Claims, One by One
lede: The audit, applied. Four are true and should be received; three are half-true and must be corrected; seven are false and must be refused. The ratio is the point.
---

A system is not refuted by being disliked. It is refuted — or received — claim by claim, and the verdicts are not all the same verdict. What follows is the audit in its most concrete form: fourteen claims that between them carry most of the manifest space's weight, each stated in the strongest form I can give it, each given a verdict, each given an argument, and each given something to do instead.

The verdicts are **integrate** (the claim is true, and the Church should say so louder), **correct** (the claim names something real but describes it wrongly), and **reject** (the claim is false in a way that cannot be repaired inside the system). The whole audit is summarized in a table at the end of the chapter; a reader who wants the shape of the result before the arguments should read that table first.

### 1. Attention and expectation shape action and perception

**Integrate.** This is not a piece of New Age metaphysics; it is a piece of Aristotelian psychology that the Church received eight centuries ago and that contemporary cognitive science has done nothing but confirm. What we attend to we perceive; what we expect we tend to find; what we rehearse we perform. Aquinas's treatise on the passions and the *vis cogitativa* — the estimative power by which we grasp a situation as "good for me" or "threatening" — is a more rigorous account of this than anything in the self-help literature, and it makes the same practical point: the interior life is the proximate cause of action.

The manifest space's error is to extend this from action to *being* — to claim that attention shapes not only what we do but what happens. That extension is false. The core is true, and a Catholic who denies it has denied the doctrine of virtue.

**What to do instead:** examine what you habitually attend to. The examen does this at the level of the day; the doctrine of *habitus* does it at the level of a life.

### 2. Gratitude changes the one who is grateful

**Integrate.** The most defensible claim in the system, and the one most likely to be dismissed by critics who have never kept the practice. Gratitude reorders attention, softens resentment, and makes a person capable of receiving. The Church has known this since the *Eucharist* — *eucharistia*, thanksgiving — and has a whole ascetical tradition built on it: the examen's first point is gratitude, not confession.

The manifest space's error is not in the practice but in the address. Its gratitude is directed at a mechanism, and a mechanism cannot be thanked. What is being performed, in a great deal of manifestation journalling, is the *form* of thanksgiving with the *object* removed — which is why the practice, at its most intense, produces a strange hollow feeling that its practitioners describe and cannot explain.

**What to do instead:** thank someone. Specifically, by name, for something specific, out loud. The general gratitude of the journal becomes the particular gratitude of the *Te Deum* and of the thank-you note, and particular gratitude is the kind that changes a person.

### 3. Words shape the speaker

**Integrate.** Speech is not the transmission of pre-existing thought; it is one of the ways thought is formed. What we say about ourselves, we come to believe; what we say about others, we come to see. James devotes a chapter to this ("the tongue is a fire"), and the tradition built an entire discipline — custody of speech — around it.

But the manifest space extends the claim in a direction the tradition refuses: from *words shape the speaker* to *words shape the world*. The extension has a name in Catholic moral theology, and it is not a compliment: it is the error of the *magus*, the belief that the correctly formulated word compels.

**What to do instead:** attend to what you say about yourself in the third person and about others in the second. Bless rather than curse, literally and out loud. And when you notice that you have been speaking a false sentence about yourself for years, go to confession and hear the true one said over you by someone with authority to say it.

### 4. Imagination mediates knowledge and desire

**Integrate.** Aquinas holds that the intellect cannot think without the phantasm — the image — and that desire is aroused by what is imagined. Ignatius built a method on it: the composition of place, the application of the senses. The Exercises are the most sophisticated technology of imagination in the Western tradition, and they are aimed at the opposite of the manifest space's goal: not the manufacture of a desired future, but the contemplation of a given one.

The manifest space's error is a category mistake about what the imagination *is*. It treats a medium of encounter as a cause of being. The imagination is the door through which grace and truth come in and through which the affections go out. It is not a pump.

**What to do instead:** imagine the Gospel. Fifteen minutes with a scene from the life of Christ, in the Ignatian manner, will do more for the imagination than a year of visualization, because the scene is real.

### 5. Desire is a guide to the good

**Correct.** Desire is not arbitrary noise; it is the soul's inclination toward the good, and it is revelatory: it tells you what you love. The tradition takes it seriously — "What do you want me to do for you?" is a question Jesus asks, and he does not treat the answer as an impertinence.

But desire is *disordered* as well as revelatory, and both halves are essential. *Spe Salvi* 33 puts it exactly: "In prayer we must learn what we can truly ask of God — what is worthy of God… We must learn to purify our desires and our hopes." The manifest space keeps the revelatory half and drops the purifying half, and the result is a person who takes her desires as instructions from the universe and has no way of asking whether they are good.

**What to do instead:** ask of each desire three questions — *what is this a desire for?* (the object beneath the object), *what would it cost someone else?*, and *would I want it if I had to receive it from God's hand rather than my own?* The third question is the one that does the work.

### 6. Persistent asking is heard

**Correct.** This is biblical, and the manifest space quotes the verses correctly: ask, seek, knock; the friend at midnight; the widow and the unjust judge. The tradition has a doctrine of impetration, and it is robust.

What the manifest space gets wrong is the *causality of prayer*. It makes asking into a technique that compels, and it makes the answer a function of the asker's state. Aquinas's account is both more sober and more encouraging: "Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed… we pray not that we may change the Divine disposition, but that we may impetrate that which God has disposed to be fulfilled by our prayers" (ST II-II, q. 83, a. 2). Prayer is a real cause, ordained as a cause, and it does not manipulate.

**What to do instead:** ask specifically, repeatedly, with the confidence of a child, and then add the clause that makes the prayer Christian: *not as I will.* And when the answer is no, do not conclude that you asked badly. Conclude that you are being given something else, and go looking for it.

### 7. You create your reality

**Reject as stated; correct to cooperation.** The sentence is attractive because it is almost true and the almost is invisible.

You do not create your reality. You act inside a reality you did not create, with a body you did not design, in a history you did not choose, under a providence you do not administer. What you do is *cooperate* — and the *Catechism* gives that cooperation a dignity that the manifest space's version actually diminishes: "God grants his creatures not only their existence but also the dignity of acting on their own" (CCC 307).

The difference is not verbal. A creator is responsible for the whole; a cooperator is responsible for her part. If you create your reality, then every disaster in your life is your fault and every disaster in your neighbour's life is hers. If you cooperate in a reality that is given, then your part is real, your responsibility is proportionate, and the parts you do not control are not your guilt.

**What to do instead:** distinguish, in any situation, what is yours to do from what is not. Then do the first with all your strength and entrust the second — which is the structure of every Catholic prayer ever written.

### 8. Everyone is you pushed out

**Reject.** Treated at length in the previous chapter, and rejected there for three reasons that bear repeating in one line each: it makes the other person a symptom rather than a subject; it makes love impossible; and it refutes itself, since the person who taught it to you was not you.

**What to do instead:** assume that the person in front of you is more real than your idea of her. Then ask her a question and listen to the answer, which is the only known method of discovering that someone exists.

### 9. The universe responds to your vibration

**Reject as stated; transpose.** There is no such thing as vibration in this sense. The word borrows the authority of physics and delivers a metaphor, and the metaphor is doing the work of a doctrine — specifically, the doctrine of providence — while denying everything that makes providence providence: that the one who orders is personal, free, and not under our control.

The transposition is real, though, and it is this: reality *is* responsive, and not merely mechanically. Grace answers prayer. Sacraments effect what they signify. The saints intercede. Providence orders events, including the free acts of creatures, without compelling them. The world is not a machine. But the responsiveness runs from a person to a person, and the initiative is not ours.

**What to do instead:** replace the question "what am I vibrating?" with the question "to whom am I speaking, and what has he promised?"

### 10. You are God; you are Christ-conscious

**Reject.** This is the claim that cannot be softened, because it is the metaphysical centre of the system, and because the Church has a true doctrine that looks similar from a distance and is the exact opposite.

The true doctrine is divinization: "The Son of God became man so that we might become God" (CCC 460, quoting Athanasius and Augustine). But the Catholic doctrine is that we become God *by grace*, by adoption, by participation, receiving a life that is not ours by nature. The manifest space's doctrine is that we are God *by nature*, and that the task is to realize it. The first is the most extravagant gift in the history of religion. The second is the oldest lie in the book: *you shall be like God.*

**What to do instead:** say the truth out loud, in the first person, once a day: *I am not God, and I am not nothing. I am a son in the Son.* The sentence is harder to believe than the lie, which is why it has to be practiced.

### 11. Illness and misfortune are attracted by the sufferer

**Reject.** The most harmful claim in the system, and the one for which there is no softening. It is refuted by John 9, by Job, and by the Cross, and it is refuted experientially by every hospital ward in the world.

There is a version of this claim that is true and modest: some illness is behaviourally mediated, some suffering is the fruit of sin, and the connections are worth studying. That version is medicine and moral theology. The manifest-space version is different in kind: it says that the interior state is the *efficient cause* of the disease, which makes the sick person the author of her illness and turns the care of the sick into an investigation.

**What to do instead:** whenever you are tempted to explain someone's suffering to them, do not. Sit down. Ask what would help. Bring food. Pray. If you must say something, say the thing that Job's friends should have said and didn't: *I don't know why, and I am not going anywhere.*

### 12. The past can be revised

**Correct.** There is a real experience here, and it is one of the most striking in the whole system. People who practise "revision" report a genuine change in their relation to a past event: the memory loses its sting, the story changes shape, the identity built on the old version becomes available for revision.

That is real. It is also not what the system says it is. The system says the past itself is altered, that the event is rewritten in the fabric of reality, that one's history becomes a different history. That is false, and it is false in a way that costs the practitioner something important: a past that can be rewritten cannot be forgiven, because forgiveness presupposes that the thing happened.

The Catholic alternative is much stronger. Confession does something to the past that no amount of reimagining can do: it removes the guilt while leaving the fact intact, and it does so by the authority of another. The healing of memories, where it is authentic, does not delete the event; it heals the wound the event left, by bringing Christ into the memory rather than replacing it.

**What to do instead:** go to confession. Name the thing. Receive absolution. Then, if the memory still wounds, pray the event with Christ present in it — not instead of it.

### 13. Signs and numbers decode the future

**Reject.** *"All forms of divination are to be rejected… Consulting horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, interpretation of omens and lots, the phenomena of clairvoyance, and recourse to mediums all conceal a desire for power over time, history, and, in the last analysis, other human beings, as well as a wish to conciliate hidden powers"* (CCC 2116). The 11:11 is an omen; it is a small one, and the Catechism's sentence covers it.

The reason the Church is severe here is not fussiness. It is that the practice trains a person to relate to reality as a code to be broken rather than a gift to be received, and it produces a permanent low-grade anxiety that is the opposite of faith. The alternative is not "ignore providence." It is discernment: the tested reading of interior movements and external events, ordered to action rather than to prediction.

**What to do instead:** when you notice a coincidence, do not decode it. Thank God for it, if it moved you to gratitude, and then do the next right thing. If you are trying to decide something, use the Ignatian rules for discernment, a trusted adviser, and the ordinary means — and then decide.

### 14. A technique guarantees the outcome

**Reject.** This is the claim that makes the rest operational, and it is the one the Church has a precise name for: *tentare Deum*, tempting God.

Aquinas treats it as a species of superstition (ST II-II, q. 97): it is the attempt to put God to the test, to require a specified result as the price of belief. And the Catechism names the underlying error with great precision: superstition "can even affect the worship we offer the true God, e.g., when one attributes an importance in some way magical to certain practices otherwise lawful or necessary" (CCC 2111).

Notice: the Catechism's target is not the pagan. It is the Catholic who treats the practice as a mechanism. Which means that the most dangerous version of this error is not in the manifest space at all. It is in the parish, in the person who says nine novenas to get the result and is quietly certain that the tenth will work if the first nine were said properly.

**What to do instead:** do the practice, then say, out loud, *thy will be done.* If the sentence sticks in your throat, you have found the thing that needs healing, and it is not the outcome.

::scene

Fourteen claims, and the tally is four integrate, three correct, seven reject.

That tally is the argument of this book in miniature. A response that rejected all fourteen would be false, unjust, and useless. A response that accepted all fourteen would be a surrender with incense. The Church's response has to be the one thing that is harder than either: discriminate, name, receive, refuse, and then build the alternative so beautifully that the refusal becomes attractive.

| Verdict | Claims | Disposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Integrate** | 1 attention and expectation · 2 gratitude · 3 words shape the speaker · 4 imagination mediates knowledge | Teach them louder, and give them their true object |
| **Correct** | 5 desire as guide · 6 persistent asking · 12 revision of the past | Keep the experience, replace the causality |
| **Reject** | 7 you create your reality · 8 everyone is you pushed out · 9 the universe answers vibration · 10 you are God · 11 illness is attracted · 13 signs decode the future · 14 technique guarantees the outcome | Name them, refuse them, and supply the alternative |

Four, three, seven. The proportion is the argument: a response that rejected all fourteen would be as false as one that accepted them, and a response that accepted the four has to *mean* it, which means teaching them better than the movement does.
