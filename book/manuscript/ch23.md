---
title: Desire and Its Purification
lede: The manifest space takes desire as an oracle. The tradition takes it as a signpost — one that must be read, tested, and purified. This chapter is about the difference, and about what happens to a person when the desire is not granted.
---

Augustine began the *Confessions* with a sentence that every spiritual writer since has had to reckon with: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."

It is a sentence about desire, and it makes three claims at once. Desire is constitutive — we are made *for* something. Desire is disordered — the heart is *restless*, which is not a compliment. And desire has an object that is not any of the things it appears to want — it rests only in God.

The manifest space affirms the first, denies the second, and denies the third. It says that desire is the compass (true), that it needs no purification (false), and that its object is the thing desired (false). Three claims, two errors, and the errors are the ones that turn a spiritual practice into a machine for producing disappointment.

::scene

Start with what is true, because the manifest space's insight here is real and the Church has often been slow to say so.

**Desire is revelatory.** What you want tells you what you love, and what you love tells you who you are. This is not a modern insight; it is the structure of the whole moral theology of the *Summa*, which begins its treatment of human action with the question of beatitude and derives everything from the fact that the will is ordered to the good (ST I-II, qq. 1–5). A person who does not know what she wants does not know herself.

**Desire is a form of prayer.** "Delight yourself in the Lord, and he will give you the desires of your heart" (Ps 37:4) is a verse the manifest space quotes constantly and the tradition reads more carefully: the promise is attached to the delight, not to the desire. But the desire is not the enemy. It is the material.

**Desire is not to be shamed.** This needs saying because a certain Catholic formation produced the impression that wanting things is faintly indecent. It is not. The Gospel is full of people who ask for things — a healing, a child, a place in the kingdom, a sign — and Jesus, who rebukes a great deal, almost never rebukes the asking.

::scene

Now the two denials.

**Desire is not authoritative.** The manifest space's move is to treat the desire as an instruction from the universe: if you want it, it is yours; the work is to clarify and release. The tradition's move is to treat the desire as a *datum* that must be interpreted. Some desires are true and point to real goods. Some are true in their object and disordered in their intensity — a legitimate love of family become an idolatry of family. Some are false in their object: the desire for revenge, for another person's spouse, for a greatness that would destroy the one who got it. And some are true at a deeper level than their surface: the desire for a promotion that is really a desire to be seen, which is really a desire for the love that only God can give.

Telling a person that her desire is an instruction is like telling her that a pain is a diagnosis. It may be; it may not; either way, someone has to read it.

**Desire is not satisfiable by its apparent object.** This is the claim that the manifest space must deny and cannot avoid, and it is the claim with the most evidence behind it. Every honest person who has obtained the thing she wanted knows what happened next: a brief satisfaction, a strange flatness, and then a new want. The manifest space calls this "expanding your vibration" and treats it as progress. The tradition calls it what it is — the infinite disproportion between a finite object and a desire that was made for the infinite — and treats it as evidence.

*Spe Salvi* 33 makes the point in the context of prayer, and it is worth quoting again because it is the whole chapter in one paragraph:

> "In prayer we must learn what we can truly ask of God — what is worthy of God. We must learn to purify our desires and our hopes. We must free ourselves from the hidden lies with which we deceive ourselves."

Note what Benedict does not say. He does not say that we should stop desiring. He says that we must *learn* what we can ask — which means that the first thing a prayer teaches us is that we were asking for the wrong things, and that this discovery is part of the answer.

::scene

How is desire purified? The tradition has a great deal to say, and three instruments do most of the work.

**Ignatian indifference.** Not apathy: a freedom from inordinate attachment such that one is not *enslaved* by any outcome. "We ought to be indifferent, not only to the choice of a state of life, but also in all that concerns our own persons, so that we do not prefer health to sickness, riches to poverty, honour to dishonour, a long life to a short one" (*SpEx* 23). This is not a counsel to want nothing. It is a discipline of holding everything in an open hand, so that the will is available to God and not captive to a result.

The manifest space has a counterfeit of this — "detachment" as a technique to make the thing arrive — and the counterfeit is exactly the thing that the real practice forbids, because the real practice is not a technique for getting what you want. It is the freedom to be given something else.

**The dark night.** John of the Cross described, with a precision that still startles, what happens when a person who has been praying with sweetness stops being given sweetness. The senses lose their consolations; the imagination loses its images; the desire loses its object; and the person is left in a darkness that feels exactly like failure. It is not failure. It is the purification of desire, and its function is to detach the desire from the *consolation* so that it can rest in the *Beloved*.

This is the most important thing in this book that the manifest space has no answer to, and it is the question that its practice cannot survive: **what do you do when the thing does not come?**

The manifest space's answer is: you did not align. The tradition's answer is: you may be in the dark night, and the dark night is a gift, and the fact that it feels like abandonment is the evidence that something is being taken away that needed to be taken away.

**The offering of desire.** The practice that turns a want into a prayer is not the suppression of the want but its *presentation*. "Lord, this is what I want. I want it very much. I am not sure it is good for me. I am not sure it is good for anyone. I am asking for it, and I am asking to be made able to receive your answer." This is a completely different act from the manifest space's scripted affirmation, and it produces a completely different person: the one is a technician of her own desire, the other is a child with a Father.

::scene

Now the pastoral cases, because this is where the doctrine has to prove itself.

**The woman who wants a child.** She has been told by the manifest space that her yearning is the signal that the child is hers, and by a certain Catholic piety that she should stop wanting it and offer it up. Both are cruel. The first sets her up for a devastation that will be interpreted as her fault; the second tells her that the deepest desire of her body is a spiritual defect. The truth is harder and kinder: the desire is good, the grief is real, the outcome is not in her hands, and God is not asking her to stop wanting — he is asking her to keep wanting *him* while she waits, and to let the wanting be a prayer rather than a program.

**The man who wants recognition.** His desire is real and its object is a lie, because what he wants is not the recognition but the love that he believes the recognition would prove. The manifest space will help him get the recognition, and he will be as empty as Daniel in the first chapter. The tradition will ask him to name the deeper want, and then to bring it where it can be answered.

**The person who has got what she wanted and feels nothing.** This is the most common outcome of a successful manifestation, and it is almost never discussed in the manifest-space literature. The tradition has a word for it — *acedia*, or the noonday demon, the sorrow that arises when the good thing does not satisfy — and a treatment: not more goods, but a re-ordering of love.

::scene

There is a last thing to say, and it is the hinge to the next chapter.

The manifest space is right that desire is central to the spiritual life, and it is right that most Christians have not been taught to desire well. Where it goes wrong is in thinking that desire is a *force* to be used rather than a *faculty* to be formed.

A force is spent. A faculty is cultivated. And the cultivation of a faculty — the slow, repeated, unglamorous transformation of what a person loves — has a name in the tradition, and it is the subject of the next chapter.
