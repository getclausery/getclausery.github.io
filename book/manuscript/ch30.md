title: Transpositions IV: Signs and Guidance
lede: The desire to be guided is not superstition; it is one of the deepest human longings. The Church has a tested discipline for it, and it is not reading numbers.
---

Of all the manifest space's practices, the reading of signs is the one most likely to be dismissed as silly and most likely to be doing real damage. Both reactions are wrong, and the second is more important.

The practice is not silly, because the desire behind it is not silly. A person who looks for signs is a person who believes that the world is addressed to her — that her life is not a random sequence but a communication. That belief is true. It is one of the things that the doctrine of providence is for. The manifest space has it, badly, and the Church has it, well, and has largely stopped teaching it.

And the practice does damage, because a life spent decoding is a life spent in low-grade anxiety, and because the code is never finished, and because a person who reads signs instead of seeking counsel will eventually read the sign she wants.

::scene

### What the Church forbids, and why

The *Catechism* is blunt: "All forms of divination are to be rejected: recourse to Satan or demons, conjuring up the dead or other practices falsely supposed to 'unveil' the future. Consulting horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, interpretation of omens and lots, the phenomena of clairvoyance, and recourse to mediums all conceal a desire for power over time, history, and, in the last analysis, other human beings, as well as a wish to conciliate hidden powers" (CCC 2116).

Two things about that sentence are worth noticing.

First, "interpretation of omens" is in the list. The 11:11 is an omen. It is a small, harmless-looking one, and it belongs to a category the Church has always treated as seriously as she treats anything: because the issue is not the size of the omen but the shape of the relationship it creates. The person who reads omens is trying to obtain knowledge of the future in order to secure power over it. That is the posture the Church refuses, and she refuses it for the person's sake.

Second, the *Catechism* diagnoses the desire under it: *a desire for power over time, history, and other human beings.* This is the same diagnosis as the one this book has been making about the manifest space from the beginning. Divination is not primarily an intellectual error; it is a strategy for managing anxiety by controlling the unknown. And it does not work, which is why it must be repeated.

::scene

### What the Church offers instead: discernment

The alternative is not "stop noticing things." It is a discipline, and it is one of the Church's great practical gifts.

Ignatius of Loyola's rules for the discernment of spirits (*SpEx* 313–336) are a method for distinguishing, in the movements of one's own interior life, what comes from God, what comes from one's own disordered affections, and what comes from the enemy. They are not a theory; they are a set of rules learned by experience, tested in the confessional and in the Exercises, and they are far more reliable than any sign.

The core of the method is a distinction between **consolation** and **desolation**.

**Consolation** is a movement that increases faith, hope, and love; that brings peace; that draws the soul toward God and toward others; that produces "quiet and peace." It can be intense or quiet, but its fruit is recognizable: it makes a person more herself, more free, more loving.

**Desolation** is the opposite: darkness, disturbance, disquiet, a pull toward what is low, a loss of hope, a sense of being alone. Ignatius is careful: desolation is not sin, and it is not a sign that God has abandoned you. It is a state to be understood and resisted, not a verdict.

And then the rules that turn the method from a mood into a discipline:

- **Never make a change during desolation.** (SE 318) The most important rule in the whole set, and the one most often broken. Decisions made in desolation are almost always wrong, because desolation distorts the field of vision.
- **In desolation, intensify the practice rather than changing course.** (SE 319–321) More prayer, not less. Stay where you are.
- **Notice the direction of the movement.** (SE 314–315) Consolation draws toward God and the good; desolation draws away.
- **Test by the fruits, over time.** (SE 316) Not by intensity, and not by the coincidence.
- **Seek counsel.** (SE 326) Not a sign. A person.

::scene

### The three times of election

Ignatius also gives a structure for the larger decisions — the ones that actually need guidance — and it is worth setting out because it is the exact alternative to a life of decoded signs.

He describes three "times" in which a good choice can be made (SE 175–188):

1. **When God moves the will directly and unmistakably.** Rare, and not to be presumed.
2. **When enough clarity is given through consolation and desolation** — the movements have left a discernible direction.
3. **In tranquility**, by reasoning it out: the pros and cons, the advice of wise people, the examination of motives, the consideration of what one would advise another person to do in the same position.

Most decisions are made in the third time. This is a crucial and liberating fact. It means that a Christian is not required to wait for a sign, and is not required to have a supernatural experience, and is not obliged to feel certain. She is required to think, to pray, to seek counsel, to examine her motives — and then to decide, and to trust that God governs the decision she makes in good faith.

That is a form of guidance far more robust than sign-reading, because it can be taught, it can be reviewed, and it does not collapse when the coincidence stops.

::scene

### The practice: the movements log

Fifteen minutes in the evening, five days a week, for a month.

Write three columns:

| Movement | Where it came from | What I did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *A sudden peace about the decision* | *Seems like consolation — came during prayer, after Communion* | *Wrote it down, did not act yet* |
| *A pull to check her social media* | *Desolation or habit? Noted, and did not do it* | *Went for a walk instead* |
| *Sharp irritation at my brother* | *Own disorder — tired, hungry* | *Apologized the next day* |

The point of the log is not to decode the future. It is to learn your own interior, which is the only instrument you have for discerning anything, and which no one has ever taught you to read.

After a month, take the log to a spiritual director or a confessor. What you will discover, in most cases, is that you have been receiving guidance all along, and that the signs you were looking for were mostly noise, and that the movements that actually mattered were quiet, and could bear being tested.

::scene

### The distinction that makes it all work

There is a difference between a **sign** and a **movement**, and it is the difference between divination and discernment.

A sign is external, ambiguous, and infinitely interpretable. A movement is internal, particular, and testable. A sign says: *something will happen.* A movement says: *this is drawing you toward love, and that is drawing you away.*

The manifest space trains people to look outward for confirmation. The Church trains them to look inward for movement, and then outward for counsel, and then upward for grace. It is a slower method. It is also the only one that produces a person who can decide.

And there is one more rule, which is not in Ignatius but runs through the whole tradition, and which is the simplest answer to the question of signs: *if you are looking for a sign about whether to do something you already know is right, the sign is the knowing.*
