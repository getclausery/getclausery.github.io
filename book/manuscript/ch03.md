---
title: A Counter-Summa
lede: Read the manifest space as a theological system rather than a set of tips, and it turns out to have every part — a God, a creation, an anthropology, a sin, a salvation, a revelation, a church, and an eschatology. The parts are wrong in a consistent way.
---

Nobody in the manifest space has written a *Summa*. They have, collectively, written one anyway.

The system has a doctrine of God, a doctrine of creation, an anthropology, a hamartiology (a doctrine of sin), a soteriology, a revelation, an ecclesiology, a sacramental theology, and an eschatology. None of these is stated as such; each is implied by the grammar described in the last chapter, and each can be extracted by asking the questions the tradition asks. Doing so is not pedantry. It is the only way to see that the manifest space is not a set of tips that happen to be attached to some odd ideas. It is a rival account of everything, with a hole where the creator should be.

Here is the extraction.

::scene

**On God.** The manifest space's God is either the universe or the self, and usually both at once. Neville Goddard is the most explicit, though the sentence he repeats most often is not his: *"Man is all imagination. God is man and exists in us and we in him"* — William Blake's annotation to Berkeley, which Neville quoted in lecture after lecture until it became the load-bearing wall of his system. What Neville adds to Blake is not a metaphysics but a *method* for using it. The identification is not a metaphor in his system; it is the system's foundation. In the energetic wing, the divine is an impersonal field of vibration, addressed in the second person out of courtesy. In the psychologized wing, the question is avoided, which is itself a theological position: a God so vague that nothing can be asked of him and nothing can be owed to him.

What is never present, in any variant, is a God who is *other*: free, personal, creator, able to say no, able to give what was not asked for, able to be loved rather than operated. The 2003 Pontifical Council document put the matter with unusual precision: the God of the New Age "is neither personal nor transcendent. Nor is it the Creator and sustainer of the universe, but an 'impersonal energy' immanent in the world, with which it forms a 'cosmic unity': 'All is one'" (*Jesus Christ, Bearer of the Water of Life*, §2.3.4.2).

**On creation.** If God is the self, creation is projection. The world is a mirror, an outpicturing, a "hardening into fact" of assumptions. Neville: "The world is yourself pushed out." This is not a poetic gloss on the doctrine of creation; it is its exact inversion. In Genesis, God creates and the creature receives; the world is given, prior, and independent of my mood. In the manifest space, the world is a secretion of the psyche, and its intractability — the fact that it so often refuses to comply — becomes a scandal to be explained away rather than a signature of its givenness.

**On the human person.** The anthropology is exalted and, at the same moment, impoverished. Exalted, because the person is divine in seed: unlimited, creative, "the operant power." Impoverished, because a being whose only real faculty is assumption, and whose only real problem is resistance, has no depth, no wound that is not a mistake, no capacity for tragedy, and no need of a saviour. The 2003 document again: *"We are co-creators, and we create our own reality. Many New Age authors maintain that we choose the circumstances of our lives (even our own illness and health), in a vision where every individual is considered the creative source of the universe"* (§2.3.4.1).

**On sin.** There isn't any. There is only "imperfect knowledge," "low vibration," "limiting belief," "misalignment." The document says it in five words: *"There is no sin; there is only imperfect knowledge."* This is the load-bearing error of the entire system, and everything cruel in it follows from this one point. If there is no sin, there is no forgiveness — only correction. If there is no forgiveness, there is no way to be relieved of the past except by denying it. And if there is no way to be relieved of the past except by denying it, then the practice of "revision" is not a quirk; it is a necessity, and it is a necessity that cannot work.

**On salvation.** Salvation is self-realization: the alignment of the interior with the desired exterior. It is achieved by technique, and it is verified by circumstance. This is Pelagianism with a user manual — indeed it is worse than Pelagianism, because Pelagius at least thought the human will was ordered to a good it had not invented. *Gaudete et Exsultate* names the modern form with precision: "justification by their own efforts, the worship of the human will and their own abilities… an excessive concern with programmes of self-help and personal fulfilment" (§57). That sentence was written about Catholics. It applies, with the polarity reversed, to the manifest space as well.

**On revelation.** "The universe is always speaking." Signs, synchronicities, numbers, songs, dreams. The claim is not that God has spoken and we must listen; it is that everything is always speaking and we must decode. The effect is a permanent, low-grade interpretive anxiety: every event is a message, every message is about me, and no message is ever finished. This is the exact opposite of the peace that the tradition associates with obedience to a revelation that has actually been given.

**On church.** There is no church, and this is not an oversight. The manifest space is constitutively solitary: one person, one notebook, one phone. Where community appears, it is a market — a coach, a course, a membership, a retreat — and its logic is the logic of the customer relationship, which is to say, the logic of someone who cannot tell you no. The 2003 document's phrase for the result is "spiritual narcissism," and it asked the question that ought to be asked of every spiritual movement: *"is it a form of self-worship?"* (§3.2).

**On sacraments.** The methods. Scripting, the 369, SATS, revision, the whisper method, the pillow method, subliminals. They are rites in the strict sense: prescribed actions, performed at prescribed times, with prescribed words, believed to produce an effect ex opere operato — by the performance. The resemblance to Catholic sacramental practice is not accidental, and it is not innocent. It is the parody that the whole book is about.

**On last things.** The eschatology is realized and material: the job, the body, the partner, the money, now. There is no death, or rather there is a death that is treated as one more thing to be reframed. This is why the system cannot survive a funeral. It has no category for the irreversible, and the irreversible is where every human life ends up.

::scene

Put like this, the system looks easy to dismiss, and dismissing it would be a mistake — for the same reason that dismissing Aristotle because he denied creation would have been a mistake. The system's errors are not random. They are the errors that a culture makes when it has lost a particular set of doctrines and retained the corresponding appetites.

Look at the list again and notice the shape.

The manifest space has an intense sense of **agency** and no doctrine of **providence**. It has an intense sense of **desire** and no doctrine of **the good**. It has an intense sense of **the interior life** and no doctrine of **sin or grace**. It has an intense sense of **signs** and no doctrine of **revelation**. It has an intense sense of **practice** and no doctrine of **the Church**. It has an intense sense of **now** and no doctrine of **the last things**.

Every one of those pairs is a Christian pair, broken in half. The manifest space is not the opposite of Christianity; it is Christianity with one half of each doctrine removed, so that the remaining half becomes monstrous. Agency without providence becomes technique. Desire without the good becomes idolatry. Interiority without grace becomes self-salvation. Signs without revelation become divination. Practice without the Church becomes a market. Now without last things becomes the denial of death.

That is the diagnosis, and it is the reason the cure cannot be a simple "no." When a person presents with half a doctrine, you do not remove the half. You supply the other half.

::scene

There is a name for the mood that results, and it is worth naming because it explains the hold the system has on intelligent people. Call it **providential atheism**: the conviction that reality is responsive, plus the conviction that nothing personal is on the other end of the response.

It is a very modern position, and it is not stupid. It is what remains when a culture retains the experience of prayer and loses the doctrine of God. The experience does not evaporate. It gets reassigned. The sense that the world is addressed to me — which is, in its origin, the sense that I am a creature — gets relocated into a law of attraction. The sense that my interior life matters beyond my interior life — which is, in its origin, the doctrine of the soul — gets relocated into "vibration." The sense that words are not neutral — which is, in its origin, the doctrine of the word and the sacraments — gets relocated into affirmations.

The result is a spirituality with all of the phenomenology of faith and none of its object. It feels, to the person inside it, exactly like religion, which is why arguments that assume irreligion will miss entirely. And it explains the strange testimony of so many people who leave the manifest space for the Church: not that they stopped having the experiences, but that the experiences suddenly had somewhere to go.

::scene

One more observation before the audit begins, because it will matter in Part Three.

The manifest space is often described as a form of materialism, and in one sense it is: it promises things, and it measures success in things. But in a deeper sense it is the opposite. It is a form of **idealism** — the doctrine that reality is at bottom mental, and that the mental is therefore the only lever worth pulling. Christianity, by contrast, is the religion in which God became flesh, in which matter mediates grace, in which water and oil and bread and wine and bodies and places and history are the chosen instruments of the divine.

Which means that, on the question of matter, the Church is *more materialist* than the manifest space, and this is one of the great unused arguments in its arsenal. The person who has been taught that everything is a projection of consciousness has not been taught to be worldly; she has been taught to be trapped in her own head, with a universe made of it. The Church offers her the scandalous alternative: a God who made a world that is not her, who entered it in a body, and who gives himself to her in things she can touch.

That is not a smaller promise than the manifest space makes. It is a larger one. But it will not be heard until the diagnosis is made in the right order — and the diagnosis is not "you are too attached to the world." It is "you have been given a universe made of yourself, and you are suffocating in it."
