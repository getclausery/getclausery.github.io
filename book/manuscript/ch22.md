---
title: Providence and Secondary Causality
lede: This is the engine room. Get this doctrine right and the manifest space loses its market; get it wrong and you produce either fatalism or magic.
---

Every spirituality has a doctrine of how things happen. The manifest space's doctrine is a law of resemblance. The Church's doctrine is providence, and providence is the most misunderstood word in popular Catholicism.

It is misunderstood in two directions at once, and both misunderstandings are common in the same parish.

**The first is fatalism**: providence as a script written in advance, in which human action is a decoration. On this view, praying is pointless because the outcome is fixed, and the only piety is acceptance. This is not the Catholic doctrine; it is a version of it that has done enormous damage, particularly to the sick and the grieving.

**The second is deism with a religious vocabulary**: providence as a general supervision, in which God set things going and now watches, and the actual working of the world is done by laws and by us. On this view, God is real but remote, and the daily texture of life is administered by second causes with no particular divine involvement.

Aquinas's doctrine is neither, and the difference is technical, and the technique matters.

::scene

Begin with the definition. Providence, for Aquinas, is the *ratio* — the plan, the ordering — of things to their end, existing in the divine mind (ST I, q. 22, a. 1). Governance is the execution of that plan (q. 103). And the crucial move comes in the next step: God governs the world *through* secondary causes, not instead of them.

"Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed" (ST II-II, q. 83, a. 2). That sentence is the whole doctrine in one line. God does not merely decide the outcome; he decides the *causal order* by which the outcome arrives, and that order includes real creatures doing real things.

The *Catechism* puts it in language a child can follow: "God is the sovereign master of his plan. But to carry it out he also makes use of his creatures' cooperation. This cooperation is not a passive one; God grants his creatures the dignity of acting on their own and of being causes for one another" (CCC 306). And then: "God grants his creatures not only their existence but also the dignity of acting on their own" (CCC 307).

Read those two paragraphs slowly, because they contain the answer to the manifest space and to fatalism simultaneously.

**Against fatalism:** your action is not a decoration. It is a cause, ordained as a cause, and the outcome genuinely depends on it. When you work, the work does something. When you ask, the asking does something. When you forgive, the forgiveness changes the world. God has chosen to govern a world in which creatures are causes, and he does not bypass them.

**Against magic:** your action is not the *only* cause, and it is not the *first* cause. It operates inside an order you did not establish, alongside causes you cannot see, under a wisdom you cannot audit. You are a real cause and you are not the cause, and the difference between those two sentences is the difference between a creature and a god.

::scene

Now the part that is hardest and most important: chance.

The manifest space cannot tolerate chance. If something happens, something caused it, and if something caused it, you can find it, and if you can find it, you can use it. A world with genuine contingency is a world in which some things happen that are not about you, and that is intolerable to a system whose entire promise is that everything is about you.

Aquinas has room for chance, and he has it precisely because he has room for real secondary causes. A thing is accidental or fortuitous *with respect to* its secondary causes — the man who happens to be at the well when the other man passes by — and it is nevertheless foreseen and ordered by God, who includes the accidents in the plan (ST I, q. 22, a. 2, ad 1; q. 116, a. 1). Chance is real at the level of creatures and not ultimate at the level of God.

This is a doctrine with immense pastoral value, and it is almost entirely absent from contemporary preaching. It means that the flat tire is not a message. It means that the illness is not a verdict. It means that the fact that two events coincided does not oblige you to find a meaning, and that the refusal to find one is not a failure of faith but an act of humility. The manifest space's sign-reading and the over-pious Catholic's sign-reading are the same error: the refusal to let anything be merely contingent.

::scene

Which brings us to the hard question, the one every pastor is asked in a hospital corridor: **if God governs, why this?**

The tradition's answer has three parts, and none of them is "God wanted this."

**First: God does not will evil.** The language of "God's will" needs to be broken into three senses, which are routinely conflated and which produce most of the cruelty in this area. There is God's *prescriptive* will — what he commands, which is always good. There is his *permissive* will — what he allows, which includes the free acts of creatures and the workings of nature, and which he allows for reasons that are often not given to us. And there is his *particular* will for an individual — the vocation, the path, the thing he is asking of you. Saying "God took your child" confuses permission with prescription, and it is not the Church's teaching; it is a pastoral malpractice with a pious accent.

**Second: God can bring good out of evil, and does.** "God permits evil only to draw a greater good from it" (CCC 324 is close to this; the tradition states it in many forms, and the *Catechism* is careful to add that we often do not see the good). This is not an explanation of the evil. It is a promise about its limits. The evil remains evil; the Cross remains a murder; and the resurrection does not make the murder acceptable, it makes it defeated.

**Third: the answer to the question is a person, not a proposition.** Job's question is answered by a tour of creation and, for us, by the Incarnation. God does not tell us why. He tells us who is with us, and he does it by being born, and suffering, and dying. Any theodicy that is more satisfying than that is less true.

::scene

There is one more consequence, and it is the one that makes this chapter the engine room of the whole book.

If providence is real, then **the outcome of your action is not your responsibility, and the action is.** This is the exact distribution of burden that the manifest space gets wrong in both directions: it makes you responsible for the outcome and it makes the action a technique. The Catholic distribution is the reverse, and it is the only one a human being can carry for a lifetime.

You are responsible for what you do: for the prayer, the work, the honesty, the courage, the fidelity. You are not responsible for what happens: for the diagnosis, the hiring committee, the interest rate, the other person's freedom, the timing of God. You are called to act as if everything depended on your action, and to trust as if nothing did, and the two together are not a contradiction but the definition of a creature who knows what she is.

That is what providence is for. Not to explain the world, but to make action possible without despair and without delusion.

::scene

The manifest space's "universe" is a second cause with the first cause deleted. It has the responsiveness of providence and none of its personhood, the order of governance and none of its freedom, the intimacy of a Father and none of his face.

The Catholic doctrine keeps all three, and it adds the one thing no system of attraction can offer: a God who is not a mechanism, who can say no, who has said no, and who in saying no to his Son said yes to us. That is not a law. It is a story, and it is the only story in which a creature can act with all her strength and then sleep.
