---
title: For the Common Good
lede: Three words at the end of 1 Corinthians 12:7 settle the purpose of every manifestation — and they are the words the manifest space has never once quoted.
---

"To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good."

The sentence is one clause long, and it is the most politically explosive thing Paul ever wrote, because it does three things at once that no spiritual market can survive.

It says that the manifestation is **given**, which removes the technique. It says that it is given **to each**, which removes the elite. And it says that it is **for the common good**, which removes the private benefit. Every one of the three is a direct contradiction of the manifest space, and together they constitute a complete alternative program.

::scene

The Corinthian community was, by the standards of the ancient world, embarrassingly gifted. They had tongues, prophecy, healing, discernment, teaching, generosity, and a lively sense that these things were evidence of their own spiritual attainment. Their worship was chaotic; their social life was stratified; their theology was a mess; and they were proud.

Paul's response is not to dampen the gifts. It is to re-describe them. A gift is not an achievement; it is a *charisma*, a grace-gift, given by the Spirit as he wills, distributed variously, and ordered — this is the new element — to the building up of the body. The body has many members, and the members do not choose their function, and no member can say to another "I have no need of you."

Three marks of an authentic manifestation follow, and they function as a test:

**Givenness.** It comes from outside; it is received; it is not a technique. The moment a manifestation can be produced on demand by a method, it has stopped being a charism and become a skill — which is not a sin, but it is a different thing, and the difference matters enormously for how we treat the person who has it and the person who doesn't.

**Diversity.** Not everyone has the same gift, and the absence of a particular gift is not a defect. This is a direct refutation of the manifest space's implicit claim that the successful life is available to anyone who applies the method correctly. In the body, some are apostles and some are hands and some are ears, and the ear does not become an eye by believing harder.

**Order to the whole.** The purpose is the *common good*, the building up of the body. A gift that does not build up is, on Paul's account, not a gift being used but a gift being consumed.

::scene

Now the contrast, which is exact.

The unit of the manifest space is **the individual**: one person, one notebook, one desired outcome, one life improved. The unit of the charismatic economy is **the body**: the gift is given to a member for the sake of the others, and its authenticity is tested by what it does to the whole.

The unit of the manifest space is **the outcome**: the thing desired, obtained. The unit of the charismatic economy is **the person**: built up, consoled, taught, healed, sent.

The unit of the manifest space is **the sealed interior**: the assumption held in secret, the wish not told to anyone. The unit of the charismatic economy is **the assembly**: the gift exercised in public, tested by others, subject to discernment.

It is not an exaggeration to say that these are two different religions. And the difference is not a matter of emphasis. It is a difference about what a human being is for.

::scene

The practical consequence is a diagnostic question, and it is one that can be asked gently, in a conversation, without a lecture.

**"Who is this for?"**

Take the person who is manifesting a spouse. Ask who the spouse is for. If the answer is "for me, to complete my life," the practice is a form of consumption dressed as hope. If the answer is "for the person he or she is, whom I would want to love well," something has already changed, and the change did not come from the technique.

Take the person manifesting money. Ask who it is for. If the answer is "so I can stop being afraid," that is a real need and it deserves a real response — but the response is providence and detachment, not a frequency. If the answer is "so I can look after my mother and give more away," the desire has already begun to take a form that the Church can bless, and the practice that follows is stewardship, not attraction.

Take the person manifesting a healing. This is the hardest case, and it needs to be handled with the greatest care, because the desire is holy and the presumption is dangerous. Ask who the healing is for. If it is for the suffering person's own relief, it is a legitimate petition and may be brought to God without embarrassment. If it is for the *manifestation of God's glory*, it is the prayer of the Gospel. And if it is for the sake of the manifester's own record of success, it has become something else, and the Church's long and painful experience with faith-healing ought to make us very slow to promise outcomes.

::scene

There is a temptation on the Catholic side at this point, and it is the mirror image of the manifest space's error. It is the temptation to make the gifts **safe** by making them clerical, ceremonial, and rare — to insist so strongly on order that the manifestation of the Spirit becomes a thing that happens to other people, in a church, at a scheduled hour, administered by a professional.

Paul's sentence forbids this. *To each is given.* Not to the Twelve. Not to the ordained. Not to the exceptional. To each. The Church has always insisted, against every attempt to confine the Spirit to an institution, that the *sensus fidei*, the charisms of the laity, the gifts of the young, the prophetic witness of the poor and the suffering are real manifestations, and that the body is crippled when they are suppressed.

The charismatic renewal is the great modern test case. It produced, and produces, real gifts of healing, prophecy, intercession, and joy; it also produced, in places, extravagance, manipulation, and a culture of spiritual one-upmanship that did real damage. The Church's response was neither to endorse everything nor to shut it down, but to do what she always does at her best: to test, to regulate, to insist on submission to the local ordinary, and to let the fruit decide over decades. That is the model for how any renewal of manifestation in Catholic life should be handled.

::scene

Now the transposition, in concrete form. The point of this chapter is not to replace private desire with public service — that would be a new legalism. It is to place private desire inside a body, where it can be healed and where it can become a gift.

**Private petition becomes intercession.** The person who wants to manifest a future spouse can begin, tonight, to pray for that person by name — even without a name — for their conversion, their safety, their healing, their vocation. This is not a trick to speed up the arrival. It is a change in what the desire *is*: from a want directed at the universe to a love directed at a person.

**Self-improvement becomes service.** The drive to become a better version of oneself is real, and the Church has a word for it — *vocation* — which locates improvement in a call rather than in a project. Ask not "what do I want to become?" but "what am I being asked to become, and for whom?"

**The vision board becomes an intention list that is prayed over and then acted on.** The difference is not in the paper. It is in the third column: for each item, write who it serves, and what the first concrete act would be. Half the items will not survive that column, and the half that does will be real.

**The secret becomes the confessional and the direction of a soul.** The manifest space's insistence on secrecy ("tell no one, or the energy dissipates") is one of its most revealing features, because it is the exact opposite of the Catholic practice of manifestation of conscience — the disclosure of one's interior to a director precisely so that it can be tested, corrected, and prayed for. What the manifest space calls protection is, functionally, isolation.

::scene

A last word on the "common good," because it is a phrase with a long history and it does not mean "charity."

The common good, in Catholic social teaching, is not the sum of private goods and not the aggregate of individual preferences. It is the good of a *people*, a good that is shared and that is only possessed together: peace, justice, worship, the flourishing of families, the care of the poor, the possibility of a good life for everyone. A manifestation is for the common good when it makes the manifester more capable of contributing to that, and less capable of being consumed by her own project.

Which is a way of saying that the test of a genuine manifestation is not whether the thing arrived. It is whether the person, in the process, became more available.

That is a standard no technique can meet, and it is the standard by which every practice in this book should eventually be judged.
