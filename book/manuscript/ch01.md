She writes it three times in the morning, six in the afternoon, nine at night. Three, six, nine. The number of the method is in the method's name, which tells you something about the kind of thing it is: not a doctrine to be believed but a procedure to be performed, and procedures are easier to love than doctrines.

The sentence she writes is in the present tense, because the present tense is the whole trick. Not *I want a job that does not make me sick with dread.* Not *I hope I meet someone.* But: *I am grateful for my new job. I am grateful for my new job. I am grateful for my new job.* Nine times, in a notebook with a cover the colour of a good decision, at 11:11, because the clock said 11:11 and the clock saying 11:11 is a sign, or it is not a sign, and either way it costs nothing to write it down.

She is twenty-four. She was baptized, confirmed, and made her first communion at a parish with a car park and a noticeboard. She can recite the Our Father and could not, if you asked her, tell you what the Church teaches about providence, because nobody ever told her, and because the question never came up in a way that made it urgent. What did come up, urgently, at 2 a.m., in the months after her mother's diagnosis, was the question of whether anything at all was listening.

She has not left the Church. She goes at Christmas and sometimes at Easter, and she has a rosary in a drawer, and she has prayed it twice. She would describe herself, if pressed, as spiritual but not especially religious, which is a sentence she learned from the internet and which describes, accurately, a person who has a practice and no theology to hold it. She is not confused about whether God exists. She is confused about whether he is *in the room*.

And so: the notebook. The 11:11. The gratitude that is also a claim. She is not doing anything wicked. She is doing something that works, in the way that a placebo works and in the way that a habit works and in the way that a person who has been told she is powerless will seize on any lever, even a lever that does not exist, because a lever in the hand is better than a void.

::scene

The manifest space is hard to see because it has no building. It is not a religion by any of the measures we use to count religions: no membership, no clergy, no creed, no census category. It is a vocabulary, a set of practices, and a mood, distributed through books and feeds and group chats, and it has become so ordinary among people under thirty-five that it functions the way "karma" functions: a word you use without committing to a cosmology.

The books are not marginal, though. *The Secret* has sold, by its publisher's count, more than thirty million copies since 2006, and its central claim — that like attracts like, that thought is a form of transmission, that the universe is a catalogue from which you order by feeling — entered the general culture so completely that it is now difficult to parody. The earlier layer, the New Thought writers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and above all Neville Goddard, whose shelf of short books and several hundred recorded lectures have been digitized, clipped, remixed, and set to ambient music by a revival that began around 2015 and has not yet crested — supplies the metaphysics. The newest layer supplies the *liturgy*: short video, first person, soft-spoken, direct address. *You are going to script it. You are going to feel it as if it has already happened. You are not going to tell anyone.*

None of this shows up in parish statistics. It shows up in confession.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been — a while. I think I've been manifesting, and I don't know if that's a sin."

::scene

**Nadia** is a composite, made of a dozen conversations, and I give her a name only so that the argument has somewhere to live. She works in marketing. She has a spreadsheet of her manifestations, colour-coded, and she is faintly embarrassed by it, which is itself interesting: she is embarrassed not by the practice but by the *hope* in it. She manifests a promotion, a specific number on a scale, and a man she has never met, whose initials she has written in the back of the notebook because a video told her that writing the initials is the step that makes it real.

Her mother is in remission. Nadia believes — and this is the sentence that should stop any pastor in his tracks — that she *caused* the remission. Not that her prayers were heard. That her frequency changed the outcome. She is not boasting. She says it the way a person says something they are afraid to say out loud, because if the thought created the remission, then the thought could have created the cancer, and she is not sure, at night, which one she was thinking in the year before the diagnosis.

**Daniel** is thirty-two and left the Church at nineteen, over a homily about Hell, and found manifestation through a business mentor. He is now, by any ordinary measure, a success: a coaching practice, an apartment, a body that suggests discipline. He is also the most honest person in this book. "It works," he told me. "That's the thing nobody wants to deal with. It works. And then you get the thing, and you discover that the reason you wanted it was that you thought it would make you feel like a person. And it doesn't. So you manifest the next thing." He has begun, in the last two years, to pray again, in secret, in a way he cannot explain and would not defend.

**Father Anselm** is fifty-eight and has been a pastor for twenty-one years. He is not a fool and he is not a liberal and he is not a reactionary; he is tired. He hears the question about manifestation perhaps twice a month. He has tried three answers. He has told a young woman that manifestation is superstition, and watched her face close. He has told another that it is "just a way of setting intentions," and felt the sentence curdle in his mouth as he said it, because he knew it was not. He has said, "bring it to prayer," and she did, and it did not stop, and she stopped mentioning it.

His problem is not that he does not know the doctrine. His problem is that the doctrine he knows is true and abstract and *slow*, and the thing he is competing with is false and concrete and *available at eleven at night*, and he has four minutes in a car park.

::scene

Here is the thesis of this book in a form that a pastor can use in the car park.

**The manifest space is not primarily a set of false beliefs. It is a set of true observations attached to a false engine.**

The observations are real. Attention does shape perception and action. Habit does harden into character. Desire is not neutral and is not arbitrary. Words do things. The interior life is not private. Expectation is a form of causality. Nobody who has actually prayed, or actually loved, or actually grieved, will deny that the inside of a person is connected to the outside of the world. The manifest space noticed this, said it loudly, and built a practice around it, at a moment when large parts of Christian catechesis had stopped noticing it at all.

The engine is false. The claim that the connection runs by *resemblance* — that the thought of a thing causes the thing, by a law, because thought is a kind of energy and like attracts like — is not an exaggeration of Christian teaching. It is a replacement of it. It makes the creature the creator, the will the efficient cause, and the outcome the product of a technique. It has more in common with the cosmology of the *Corpus Hermeticum* than with the Gospel, and it produces, inevitably, a moral order in which the sick are the authors of their illness.

So the question for the Church is not: *is this true or false?* It is partly true and structurally false, which is the hardest combination, because a thing that is partly true can survive refutation indefinitely. The question is: **what would it take to separate the observation from the engine, and give people the observation back with a better engine?**

That is, more or less, the question Thomas Aquinas faced in the 1260s, with different material.

::scene

Three costs make this urgent. They are not the costs usually named.

The first is **the blame of the sufferer.** A system in which interior states cause exterior circumstances has no way to account for innocent suffering, and so it must deny that suffering is innocent. The logic is not optional; it is internal. If you attracted the good, you attracted the bad. The manifest space tries to soften this with talk of "lessons" and "growth," but the softness is cosmetic. Somewhere in the system's basement there is a room in which a woman is told that she attracted her child's leukemia, and the room is not empty.

The second is **the evacuation of prayer.** When a practice becomes a technique, the other person disappears. A technique addresses a mechanism; a prayer addresses a Father. The manifest space trains people, with great efficiency, to relate to the universe as a mechanism — and then those same people come to Mass and relate to God the same way, and find that it does not work, and conclude either that God is not there or that they are doing it wrong. This is the most pastorally destructive effect, and the least discussed, because it is invisible: nobody leaves the Church over manifestation. They just stop expecting anything from prayer.

The third is **a rival soteriology with better user experience.** The manifest space offers salvation — from powerlessness, from shame, from the sense of being a passenger in one's own life — and it offers it in a form that is immediate, free at the point of use, aesthetically beautiful, and designed for a phone. The Church offers salvation too, and it is better, and it is slower, and it is administered by people who are tired and in a car park. This is not a fair fight, and pretending otherwise is not piety; it is wishful thinking.

::scene

The rest of this book is the long answer. But the first move has to be the one that any honest encounter requires, and it is the move the Church has made badly for sixty years: **describe the thing.**

Not the thing you are afraid of. Not the thing the newspaper says. Not the thing that is easy to refute. The actual thing: what it claims, in what order, with what internal logic, with what implicit theology, and with what real human need at its centre.

That is the subject of the next two chapters. And the reason to do it carefully — the reason not to skip ahead to the verdict — is that the verdict you reach without a description is always the same verdict, which is *no*, and the Church has already tried *no* as a strategy, in 1277 and in 1959, and it did not work either time.
