This chapter is written for the person in the car park.

It assumes no expertise, no library, and no time. It assumes a real person in front of you who has asked a real question, and about four minutes in which to answer it without doing harm.

::scene

### The first rule: do not begin with the metaphysics

The temptation, for anyone who has read a book like this one, is to begin with the doctrine. Do not. The person in front of you has not asked a doctrinal question. She has asked whether she is allowed to keep doing the thing that is keeping her sane.

Begin with the practice, and with her. Ask what she does, when, and what it does for her. Listen for two minutes without correcting anything. You will learn more in those two minutes than in an hour of explanation, and — this matters more than the information — she will learn that she has been heard, which is the first thing the manifest space gave her and the first thing the Church usually fails to give.

::scene

### The two questions

Almost all the diagnostic work is done by two questions. Ask them gently, and in this order.

**1. "What do you think this practice is doing?"**

The answer tells you the metaphysics. If she says "it helps me focus and keeps me hoping," she has a practice with a thin theory, and the work is catechesis and a better practice. If she says "it makes things happen," she has the system, and the work is longer. If she says "it's just a bit of fun," and then cannot stop, you have learned something else again.

**2. "If it stopped working tomorrow, what would you do?"**

The answer tells you the god. If she says "I'd be devastated, but I'd still have Mass and my family," the practice is not ultimate. If she says "I don't know — I'd have nothing," you are not dealing with a hobby. You are dealing with the thing she is actually trusting, and it is not God. That is a conversation about faith, not about superstition, and it should be had with great gentleness and over months.

::scene

### The three colours

**Green — ordinary and good.** Goal-setting; journalling; gratitude lists; positive self-talk about one's own capacities; habit and sleep routines borrowed from the self-help world; "manifesting" used as a colloquial word for hoping. *Response:* affirm it, and quietly supply the theological frame. Do not pathologize. A young person who writes down her goals is doing something the tradition calls *studiositas*, and she should be encouraged.

**Amber — ambiguous; needs catechesis and a substitute.** Visualization of a desired good; affirmations; vision boards; "manifesting" a job or a relationship in a strong sense; crystals as aesthetics; astrology as entertainment; "energy" language used loosely. *Response:* distinguish the practice from the metaphysics; give the transposition (chapter 27–30); supply a concrete Catholic practice, with instructions; check in after a month. The key sentence: *"Almost everything you're doing is fine. The reason you were given for why it works is not, and here is a better one."*

**Red — renounce, and replace.** Channeling or "higher self" guidance; consulting mediums; attempts to control another person's will; "reality shifting"; curses and hexes; the claim that illness is self-caused *used on a sick person*; any practice that requires secrecy from the Church, from a spouse, or from a director; anything involving a fee for spiritual power. *Response:* name it, briefly and without disgust; sacramental confession; and then — this is the part that is usually forgotten — *put something in the space*. A person who is told to stop and given nothing will return to the practice within a fortnight.

::scene

### The six cases

**The teenager.** She is manifesting her exams, a boy, and her mother's health. Do not take the practice away; take it *up*. Teach her the Novena of Desire, or the examen, or the Jesus Prayer, and let her keep the notebook — the notebook is not the problem, the addressee is. Give her something to do at 11:11.

**The scrupulous person.** She is terrified that her intrusive thoughts are attracting disasters, and she has begun to confess the thoughts themselves. This is one of the most urgent cases, because the manifest space's metaphysics *manufactures* scrupulosity. The doctrinal correction is precise and liberating: thoughts are not creative; temptation is not sin; the first movement is not consent. Say it plainly, repeat it, and if the scrupulosity is severe, involve a clinician — this is not a failure of faith, and treating it is not a lack of trust.

**The grieving person.** Someone has told her that she attracted the death, or that she did not manifest hard enough. Do not argue with the person who told her; argue with the sentence, gently, and then bury it. The medicine is John 9, Job, and the Cross, and the manner is silence. Say: *"That is not true, and it is not what the Church teaches, and I am sorry that you were told it."* Then sit down.

**The person in an abusive situation.** If the manifest teaching is being used to keep her in the situation — "you attracted this," "change your vibration and he will change" — the pastoral priority is her safety, not her metaphysics. The Church's teaching on the dignity of the person and the reality of sin is the doctrinal ground for leaving; it is not a failure of forgiveness to be safe. Refer to the appropriate services. This is a case where getting the doctrine right has immediate, physical consequences.

**The person refusing treatment.** She has stopped taking the medication, or has decided against the surgery, because "affirming the illness" would only manifest more of it, or because she is waiting for the healing she has been visualizing. This is not a metaphysical disagreement; it is a medical situation with a doctrinal component, and the doctrinal component has a short answer: the ordinary means of preserving life are to be used, and using them is not a failure of faith but the acceptance of a created good. The sacrament of the sick is given *alongside* the doctor — James says to call for the elders, not to dismiss the physician. Say the doctrinal sentence once, and then say the practical one: *"Take the medicine, and keep praying."* If she is a minor, or confused, or frightened in a way that is preventing her from thinking clearly, involve her family and her doctor the same day. Do not let a conversation about metaphysics delay the phone call.

**The person who has had an experience.** She prayed, or she visualized, and something happened — a healing, a coincidence, a word. Do not dismiss it, and do not endorse it as supernatural without examination. The Church's norms exist for exactly this: test the fruit, look for doctrine, ask about the guidance, and be slow. *Nihil obstat* — "for now, nothing stands in the way" — is a real and useful verdict, and it is not the same as approval.

::scene

### The rule about the bedside

There is one situation in which all of this should be set aside, and it is the one that matters most.

**Do not teach, correct, or catechize a person who is dying, or a person in acute grief, or a person in the first days of a catastrophic diagnosis.**

At the bedside, the only doctrine is presence. Sit down. Ask what would help. Bring food. Pray if you are asked, and if you are not, be quiet. Say, if it is true and if it is the right moment, that God is not punishing her, that she did not cause this, that she is loved. Say it once. Do not argue.

Everything in this book is for the weeks and months afterwards, in the ordinary time, with a person who is not drowning. Used at the bedside, the best doctrine in the world becomes another weight.

::scene

### What to hand over

Leave the person with three things, if you can.

**One practice.** Small, specific, repeatable, and written down. "Say this, at this time, for this many days." Not a reading list.

**One name.** A person she can talk to — a director, a confessor, a religious sister, a lay chaplain. The manifest space's strength is that it is always available. The Church's weakness is that the person who could help her is available on Thursdays. If you can give her a name, you have done more than a chapter of this book.

**One sentence.** The thing she will remember at 2 a.m. Choose it carefully. Mine, if I had to choose one for every case in this chapter, would be: *You did not do this, and you are not being punished, and God is not disgusted by you.*

::scene

Pastoral care is not the application of a system to a case. It is the art of being with a particular person at a particular moment in a way that makes it possible for her to receive what God is giving. Everything in this part of the book — the transpositions, the rite, the triage — is scaffolding for that, and the scaffolding should be taken down when the building is up.

The last word belongs to the tradition, and it is the oldest rule of all: *accompany, do not control.* The manifest space's great sin is that it offers control. The Church's great gift is that she offers company. And company, unlike control, can be given by anyone in a car park with four minutes to spare.
