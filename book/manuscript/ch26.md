---
title: The Grammar of Catholic Practice
lede: Six marks distinguish a Catholic practice from a technique with a Christian veneer. Every proposed transposition should be tested against all six.
---

A practice is not Catholic because it has a saint's name attached or because it is performed in a church. The manifest space has already proved that you can attach holy words to a mechanism and produce something that looks devout and is not. What makes a practice Catholic is a structure, and the structure can be specified.

There are six marks. They are not arbitrary; each one follows from a doctrine established in Part Three. A practice that lacks one of them may still be good, but it is not yet Catholic in the full sense, and if it lacks three it has become something else.

::scene

**1. It is addressed to a person.**

The doctrine: the *analogia entis*, and the personal God of revelation. A Catholic practice says *you*, not *it*. It speaks to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit — or to a saint, or to the Blessed Virgin, who is a person and who can be asked. It does not address a field, a frequency, a law, or the universe.

This is the mark that most clearly separates a Catholic practice from a manifest one, and it is not a matter of vocabulary. A person can say "Lord" and address a mechanism; a person can say "the universe" and address a Father. The test is whether the practice can survive an answer that is *no*. A practice addressed to a person can. A practice addressed to a mechanism cannot, because a mechanism that does not respond is simply broken.

**2. It is mediated by the Church.**

The doctrine: the Mystical Body and the sacramental economy. A Catholic practice is not a private invention. It comes from the Church's tradition, it is done in communion, and where it involves the sacraments it involves a minister with authority.

This does not mean that every prayer must be liturgical. It means that the practice has a *provenance*: someone gave it to you, and the someone stands in a line that goes back. The manifest space's practices have no provenance; they have an author, who is usually selling something. The difference shows up in the corrections: a tradition can correct its practices, a market cannot.

**3. It is embodied.**

The doctrine: the Incarnation and the resurrection of the body. A Catholic practice involves the body: kneeling, standing, walking, fasting, singing, touching water, eating bread, holding beads, making a sign on the forehead. Not because the body is a machine for producing spiritual states, but because the body is part of the person and cannot be left out of the person's prayer.

This is the mark that makes Catholic practice *resistant* to the manifest space's idealism. You cannot do the Rosary in your head alone for very long; the beads bring you back. You cannot fake a pilgrimage. The body keeps the practice honest.

**4. It is rhythmic.**

The doctrine: providence and the liturgical year. A Catholic practice has a *time*: daily, weekly, annually. It is not performed when one feels like it, or when a need arises, but on a schedule that is older than one's feelings — which is precisely the point, since the feelings will not cooperate.

The manifest space has methods, not rhythms. Its practices are deployed when a desire appears, and abandoned when the desire is satisfied or the technique fails. A rhythm is different: it forms the person whether or not the person is getting anything out of it, which is what formation means.

**5. It is non-manipulative.**

The doctrine: grace, and the freedom of God. A Catholic practice does not compel. It asks, it offers, it waits. It is compatible with the answer "no," "not yet," and "something else."

This is the most important mark and the most easily faked. A novena can be prayed as a technique; a Rosary can be recited as a spell; a pilgrimage can be undertaken as a transaction. The Church's own tradition warns about this constantly — CCC 2111 on superstition, the prophets on sacrifice without obedience, the whole critique of "vain repetition." The test is not the form of the words but the posture of the one saying them, and the posture is revealed by what happens when the request is refused.

**6. It is ordered to communion.**

The doctrine: the common good, and the nature of the Church as a body. A Catholic practice makes the practitioner more available to others, or it is not finished. This is why the Church's practices are almost all *public* in their orientation even when they are private: the Divine Office is prayed on behalf of the whole Church; the Rosary is a share in the prayer of the communion of saints; fasting is ordered to almsgiving; the examen ends in a resolution about how to love better tomorrow.

The manifest space's practices are complete in themselves: the practitioner and the outcome. A Catholic practice is never complete in itself, because there is always a body to which it is ordered.

::scene

Now the inventory, briefly, because most Catholics know the names and not the grammar.

**The Mass** is the paradigm of all six: addressed to the Father, mediated by the Church and the ordained minister, embodied in gesture and matter, rhythmic (daily and weekly), non-manipulative (it is a sacrifice of praise, not a transaction), and ordered to communion (it *is* communion).

**The Liturgy of the Hours** is the Church's public prayer, and it is the single most underused resource in the Catholic world. A layperson can pray Morning Prayer and Night Prayer in ten minutes a day and be inserted into a rhythm that the Church has kept for centuries. It is also, incidentally, the best available answer to the manifest space's morning and evening routines: it has the same shape — a fixed time, a fixed form, a text given to you — with the right addressee.

**The Rosary** is a rhythmic, embodied, non-manipulative contemplation of the Gospel, and its structure is genius: the body does something simple so that the mind can do something difficult.

**The examen** is the Church's instrument for noticing the movements of the interior life, and it is the direct counterpart of the manifest space's journalling.

**Lectio divina** is the Church's method for reading Scripture as address rather than information, and it is the counterpart of scripting: the same act of writing down a sentence and letting it enter, with the direction reversed.

**Fasting and almsgiving** are the Church's disciplines for the training of desire, and they are the counterpart of the manifest space's "detachment," with the crucial difference that they cost something real.

**The novena** is a nine-day petition, and it is the closest thing the Church has to a manifestation method — which is why it is the subject of chapter 31.

**Sacramentals and the liturgical year** supply the material and temporal texture that the manifest space supplies with crystals and moon phases.

::scene

A last word on invention.

None of this means that no new practice can be created. The Church's practice has always developed: the Rosary is a medieval development; the Divine Mercy chaplet is a twentieth-century one; the *Exercises* were a sixteenth-century innovation; the novena form is early modern. The tradition is alive, and a living tradition invents.

But it invents *inside the grammar*. A new Catholic practice will have the six marks, will be submitted to authority, will be tested by fruit, and will be given away rather than sold. That is the difference between development and the thing that happened in the manifest space — which invented a whole liturgy with none of the marks, no provenance, no oversight, and a price list.

The transpositions in the next four chapters are offered in that spirit: not as a new revelation, but as the old grammar applied to a new set of hungers. They should be tested, corrected, and, where they fail, replaced.
