title: The Forty Disputed Questions, I
lede: Questions 1–20, in the scholastic form but compressed — each with the objection that gives it force, the distinction that answers it, and what follows in practice. They are offered as a skeleton for a larger work, and for argument.
---

*The question is stated; the strongest objection is given in italics; the determination follows; and each ends with the practice that follows from the answer. Where a question is treated at length elsewhere in the book, the chapter is given.*

::scene

**1. Whether the universe can hear?**

*It seems that it can, since prayers are answered and coincidences cluster around intention.* The universe is a creature, and a creature is not a person. What "hears" is the one who made it. The experience of being heard is real; the addressee has been misidentified. Hearing requires a knower, and a knower requires a person or an angel, not a totality that includes the one praying. **Practice:** address the maker, not the made.

**2. Whether the universe is God?**

*It seems so, since God is everywhere and all is one.* Pantheism confuses the Creator with the creature and makes God subject to change, composition, and death. The world is in God "by essence, presence, and power," not as a part of him. **Practice:** when you are moved by a landscape, thank the painter; do not drink the paint.

**3. Whether God is an energy?**

*It seems so, since the scriptures speak of power going out from him.* "Energy" in the modern sense is a measured quantity of a physical system. God is not a quantity, is not measured, and is not part of any system. The tradition's *energeia* means operation or act, and the divine energies are God himself, not a force he emits. **Practice:** stop trying to feel the current; start trying to keep the commandments. (Ch. 9.)

**4. Whether God can be manipulated by technique?**

*It seems so, since the Church has sacraments, which work ex opere operato.* The sacraments are promises, not levers; their efficacy is God's fidelity, and they are ordered to the covenant. A technique controls its object; a promise binds the one who makes it. To treat a sacrament as a technique is the sin of superstition (CCC 2111). **Practice:** receive, do not operate. (Ch. 20.)

**5. Whether God wants you to be rich?**

*It seems so, since he promises abundance.* He promises himself, and he promises enough. Wealth is a good when it serves and a curse when it rules; the Gospel's warnings about riches are not rhetorical. The prosperity gospel mistakes a promise of the kingdom for a promise of the market. **Practice:** ask for what you need, give away what you do not. (Ch. 17.)

**6. Whether God's will can be known from signs?**

*It seems so, since Gideon asked for a fleece and got one.* Signs are ambiguous and infinitely interpretable, and the desire to read them is a desire for control over the future (CCC 2116). God's will is known through his commands, through the discernment of movements, through counsel, and through the ordinary use of reason. **Practice:** ask for counsel, not for codes. (Ch. 30.)

**7. Whether the world is a projection of consciousness?**

*It seems so, since "the world is yourself pushed out" and since perception is constructed.* Perception is indeed constructed; reality is not. The world is a creature with its own givenness, which is why it can resist you, surprise you, and outlive you. A projection cannot be loved, and the world can. **Practice:** go outside and let something be other than you. (Ch. 18.)

**8. Whether you create your reality?**

*It seems so, since attention shapes action and action shapes circumstance.* You cooperate in a reality you did not create; you are a real cause inside an order you did not establish (CCC 306–307). The difference is responsibility: a creator is responsible for the whole, a cooperator for her part. **Practice:** distinguish what is yours to do. (Ch. 13, claim 7.)

**9. Whether you are God?**

*It seems so, since the scriptures say "you are gods" (Ps 82:6) and the Church speaks of divinization.* By nature, no; by grace, yes — and the two are not close. Divinization is a gift received, which makes the creature more itself; the claim to be God by nature is the oldest lie, and it makes the creature a rival rather than a son. **Practice:** say "I am not God, and I am not nothing" once a day. (Ch. 13, claim 10.)

**10. Whether the self is an illusion?**

*It seems so, since the mystics speak of the dissolution of the self and neuroscience finds no fixed centre.* The self is not an illusion; it is a relation. It exists as constituted by God, as a body, as a member of a people, as one who is known. What is dissolved in contemplation is the *false* self — the self constructed by grasping — not the person God made. **Practice:** let yourself be known by someone; that is where the self is. (Ch. 12.)

**11. Whether everyone is you pushed out?**

*It seems so, since our assumptions shape how others appear to us and respond to us.* Our assumptions shape our perception and our conduct, and therefore some of our experience. They do not constitute other people, who are creatures with their own freedom and their own standing before God. The doctrine destroys love by destroying the other. **Practice:** ask a question and listen to the answer. (Ch. 12.)

**12. Whether human beings have a "higher self"?**

*It seems so, since we experience a self that judges the self, and the tradition speaks of the ground of the soul.* What the tradition names is conscience, the *imago Dei*, and the indwelling Spirit — none of which is a part of the self, and all of which are other. The "higher self" is a way of domesticating the voice of God so that it cannot command. **Practice:** obey what accuses you, and do not identify it with yourself. (Ch. 14.)

**13. Whether the body is a message from the mind?**

*It seems so, since stress causes illness and the psychosomatic is real.* Some illness is behaviourally and psychologically mediated, and medicine studies it. The claim that the body *is* a message, and that illness is therefore unexpressed emotion, converts a partial truth into a total explanation and makes the sick the author of their sickness. **Practice:** treat the body as a creature to be cared for, not a text to be decoded. (Ch. 11.)

**14. Whether desire is a reliable guide?**

*It seems so, since desire is natural and the Psalms ask God for what they want.* Desire is revelatory and unreliable: it tells you what you love, and what you love may be false. It requires interpretation and purification, which is what the spiritual life is for. **Practice:** ask the three questions — the object beneath, the neighbour, and the open hand. (Ch. 23.)

**15. Whether it is wrong to want things?**

*It seems so, since the saints speak of detachment and the world passes away.* It is not wrong to want; it is wrong to be possessed by wanting. The will is made for the good, and the good includes created goods; what must be purified is the *inordinateness*, not the desire. A Christianity that forbids desire produces either despair or hypocrisy. **Practice:** want boldly, and hold openly. (Ch. 23.)

**16. Whether detachment is a technique?**

*It seems so, since the manifest space teaches "let go and it will come."* Detachment as a technique for obtaining the thing is the precise opposite of detachment: it is attachment wearing a disguise. Ignatian indifference is freedom from the outcome, not a method for securing it. **Practice:** test your detachment by what happens when the thing is refused.

**17. Whether you can want something too much?**

*It seems so, since the saints speak of disordered affections.* Yes — and the sign is not the intensity of the desire but its position: what you are willing to do, and to sacrifice, and to believe, in order to have it. An inordinate desire is one that has become a god. **Practice:** name what you would not do to get it, and check whether you would still do it.

**18. Whether God gives us the desires of our hearts?**

*It seems so, since Psalm 37:4 says exactly that.* The promise is attached to delight in God: *delight yourself in the Lord, and he will give…* The desires of a heart that delights in God are different desires from those of a heart that does not, because the delight reforms the desire. **Practice:** pray for the delight before the desire. (Ch. 23.)

**19. Whether the imagination creates reality?**

*It seems so, since Neville Goddard and the whole tradition of imaginative prayer.* The imagination is a real power and a real medium of presence; it is not a cause of being. It mediates what is given; it does not give what is not. **Practice:** imagine the Gospel, where the object is real. (Appendix A, q. 19 in the longer form; ch. 27.)

**20. Whether thoughts are things?**

*It seems so, since we speak of "the power of thought."* Thoughts are acts of a spiritual power; they are real and they have effects — on the thinker, on her character, on her conduct. They are not substances and they do not act at a distance. The confusion of "real" with "physical" and of "efficacious" with "creative" is the whole error. **Practice:** take your thoughts to confession, not to the universe.
