title: What This Book Promises
---

Most books promise more than they deliver, and the blurbs are written by people who are not the author. Here is a promise written by the author, in advance, in the plainest terms available — so that you can hold the book to it, and so that you can put it down if it is not the book you need.

**If you are a pastor, teacher, catechist, or spiritual director**, this book promises you the ability to answer the question — *is it okay if I manifest?* — in about ninety seconds, without contempt and without lying, and then to hand the person something to do on Monday. The ninety seconds are in chapter 32. The something is in chapters 25 to 32, and one of them is a rite you can give away.

**If you practice manifestation, or have tried to**, this book promises not to tell you that you were stupid. It promises to take the thing you noticed seriously, to locate exactly where the mechanism you were handed goes wrong, and to argue that what you were reaching for is real, older, larger, and stranger than the technique — and that it can survive the thing the technique cannot survive, which is a refusal.

**If you are a theologian, a scholar, or a sceptic**, this book promises the strongest version of the argument, the sources for its claims, a stated method with four acknowledged limits, and the criteria by which it should be judged a failure. Chapter 36 is written to make the book falsifiable. Appendix A contains the forty questions where the real argument belongs.

**If you are none of these** — if you picked this up because someone sent you a chapter, or because the word in the title caught at something — this book promises that no chapter depends on a technical term introduced three hundred pages earlier, and that you can begin almost anywhere.

::scene

**What it does not promise.** It does not promise that manifestation works, in the sense in which its teachers claim it works. It does not promise that the Church has a ready-made answer gathering dust in a curial drawer. It does not claim authority: no bishop asked for this book, no imprimatur attaches to it, and a reader who finds an error should assume the error is the author's rather than the Church's. And it does not promise a resolution to the hardest question in the book, which is the question of why some prayers are answered and others are not; it promises only to state that question honestly and to refuse the two answers that are comfortable and false — that God is a mechanism, and that nothing is asked of us.

::scene

**What it costs you.** Forty chapters. At ten minutes a chapter, the main argument is about seven hours — a long evening a week for two months, or a weekend and a half if you are the kind of person who reads a book the way other people take a course. The short path is three hours: chapters 1, 2, 3, 13, 16, 22, 26, 31, and 32, then the conclusion. The triage-only path is forty minutes: chapter 32 and Appendix B.

**What it asks of you.** Only what any argument asks: that you hold your objection in mind while the case is being made, and that you let it be answered before you decide it has not been.

::scene

One more thing, and it is the reason for writing a book rather than a pamphlet. The claim being made here is not that a set of propositions is true — although that is part of it. The claim is that a *practice* is true, and practices cannot be argued into existence. They have to be described, taught, tested, and handed on by people who have done them. That is a work of years, and it is the work this book is trying to start. If you finish it and do nothing, the argument will have failed in the only way that matters.
