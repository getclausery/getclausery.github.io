---
title: Transpositions I: Word and Image
lede: Scripting, affirmations, visualization, vision boards, and the "I AM." Five practices with real power and the wrong addressee — and what to do with each of them tonight.
---

The manifest space's most transferable material is its *technology of the word and the image*. This is not an accident. Speech and imagination are the two powers by which human beings most directly participate in their own formation, and every spiritual tradition has developed techniques for them. The manifest space developed good techniques and pointed them at a mechanism.

What follows is not a critique but a set of substitutions, each with instructions specific enough to be tried. They should be tried badly, and then better.

::scene

### 1. Scripting → the written petition

**What the manifest space does.** Writes the desired outcome in the present tense, repeatedly, as though it had already happened, in order to impress the subconscious and align with the outcome.

**What is true in it.** Writing fixes the mind; the present tense concentrates the desire; the act of writing is an act of attention, and attention is formative. All of that survives.

**What must change.** The addressee, and the tense. A petition is written *to* someone, and it is written in the tense of a request, not of a possession. The present tense is the one thing that must go, because "I am grateful for my new job" said about a job one does not have is not faith; it is a small lie repeated until it dulls.

**The practice.** Take a sheet of paper. Write at the top: *"Father, this is what I want, and I am asking you for it."* Then write the thing plainly, in the first person, in the present tense of desire: *"I want work that does not make me sick with dread."* Then — this is the part that distinguishes it from a wish list — write three further lines:

- *What I think this would give me* (the desire beneath the desire)
- *Who else this would affect, and how*
- *What I will do this week that is mine to do*

Then sign it with a date, and put it somewhere you will find it again. Once a month, take it out and write underneath what has happened. Do not throw away the ones that were not answered. They are the most important part of the record.

**Why it works.** It converts a technique into a relationship, and it forces the three questions — the object, the neighbour, and the action — that the manifest version is designed to skip.

::scene

### 2. Affirmations → aspirations

**What the manifest space does.** Repeats a sentence about oneself ("I am worthy," "I am abundant," "I am loved") in order to reprogram the subconscious.

**What is true in it.** Repetition forms the soul. The tradition has always known this, and has a whole genre for it: the *aspiration*, the short ejaculatory prayer, said many times a day, often while doing something else. "Jesus, have mercy on me." "My Lord and my God." "Jesus, I trust in you." "Mother of God, pray for me."

**What must change.** The subject of the sentence. An affirmation is a claim about me, asserted to no one. An aspiration is a claim about God, addressed to him. The first is a form of self-suggestion; the second is a form of love, and it changes the one who says it for the opposite reason: not because repetition impresses the subconscious, but because attention to God is the thing that makes a person.

There is one affirmation that must be refused on principle, and it is the one the manifest space loves best: *"I AM."* In the tradition this is not a technique of self-realization; it is the divine name given to Moses (Ex 3:14), which is why the evangelist has Jesus say it and why the crowd picks up stones. To take the name of God and use it as a self-esteem formula is not a small confusion of categories. It is the oldest error in the book, and it is worth naming clearly because it appears, with great sincerity, in a lot of Catholic-adjacent content.

**The practice.** Choose one short scriptural line — no more than eight words — and say it at three fixed moments: when you wake, at midday, and when you lie down. Say it out loud if you can. Do not evaluate whether it is "working." It is not a technique; it is a conversation, and the measure is not the feeling but the fidelity.

::scene

### 3. Visualization → composition of place

**What the manifest space does.** Imagines the desired outcome in vivid sensory detail, from the first person, with the feeling of having received it.

**What is true in it.** Imagination is a real cognitive power and a real form of presence. The intensity, the sensory detail, the first-person perspective — all of this is exactly right, and the manifest space has, without knowing it, reinvented a technique that Ignatius of Loyola codified in 1548.

**What must change.** The object. Visualization imagines a future that does not exist and tries to make it real. Composition of place imagines a mystery that *does* exist and tries to enter it.

**The practice.** Fifteen minutes. Choose a Gospel scene — the storm on the lake, the woman at the well, the road to Emmaus, the raising of Lazarus. Then, in Ignatius's manner:

1. **See the place.** Build it in detail: the light, the water, the dust, the crowd. (*SpEx* 47)
2. **Hear the sounds.** The wind, the voices, the silence.
3. **Smell and taste.** The fish, the fire, the wine.
4. **Touch.** The wood of the boat, the dust on your feet.
5. **Then enter it.** Do not watch from outside. Be a person in the scene. Notice who is looking at you.
6. **Speak.** Ignatius ends every contemplation with a *colloquy* — a conversation, as one friend speaks to another, or as a servant to a master, or as a child to a parent. Say something. Then wait.

**Why it works.** It gives the imagination what it was made for — the presence of God in his mysteries — and it produces the thing visualization can only counterfeit: the experience of being addressed.

::scene

### 4. Vision boards → the altar, the icon, the Mass intention

**What the manifest space does.** Assembles images of desired outcomes and looks at them daily to focus attention and attract.

**What is true in it.** The visual environment shapes desire. We become what we look at. The Church has always known this, which is why it built cathedrals.

**What must change.** What is looked at, and what it is for. A vision board is a portrait of a future self; an altar, an icon corner, or a crucifix is a portrait of God and of the saints, and the difference is not a matter of taste. The board says *this is what I will have*. The icon says *this is who is with me*.

**The practice.** Make a small physical place in your home — a shelf, a corner, a nail — and put on it: a crucifix, one icon or holy card, a candle, and a slip of paper with the intention you are currently praying for. Change the paper when the intention changes. Light the candle when you pray. If you want to keep the visual discipline of a board, make the board an *intercession* board: photographs of the people you are praying for, with their names.

And then add the practice that has no manifest equivalent, and which is worth more than all of the above: have a Mass offered for your intention. It costs nothing, it involves a priest, it is offered on an altar, and it is the most powerful thing the Church can do for a person — and it is not a technique, because it depends on Christ and not on you.

::scene

### 5. "Name it and claim it" → the name of Jesus

**What the manifest space does.** Speaks a desired outcome as though it were already granted, on the model of the Word-of-Faith preachers: "I declare," "I decree," "I claim."

**What is true in it.** There is real power in the name of Jesus — "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Phil 2:10) — and the Church has never been embarrassed by it. The Jesus Prayer, the invocation in deliverance, the naming of the name in the litany: all of this is real.

**What must change.** Everything about the grammar. The name is not a password. It is the name of a person, and it is powerful because the person is, not because the syllables are correct. A decree issued to the universe is magic; a name invoked in faith is prayer. The difference is the same difference as everywhere else in this book: whether there is someone there.

**The practice.** Learn the Jesus Prayer — "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner" — and say it for ten minutes, with the breath, sitting still. Then use it in the moment of fear, in the moment of temptation, in the moment of the 3 a.m. waking. It is the shortest, oldest, and most portable practice in the Christian tradition, and it has been the whole spiritual life of millions of people who could not read.

::scene

The common thread in all five is the same thread as in the rest of this book: keep the technique, change the addressee. The manifest space is not wrong that words and images form a person. It is wrong about what they are for, and about who is listening.

And the proof that the change is not merely semantic is what happens when the request is refused. The technique has nothing to say. The prayer has somewhere to go.
