An argument is not a programme. The first four parts of this book have tried to establish what is true; this part asks what would have to be *built* if the truth were taken seriously at the scale at which it is needed.

The precedent is instructive here for a reason that is easy to miss. Thomas did not win the thirteenth century by writing better arguments than the Averroists. He won it by founding a school, persuading an order, attaching a curriculum to a university, and outlasting his own condemnation by half a century. The instruments were institutional: translations, chairs, disputations, quodlibetal questions, sums, and the slow machinery of canonization. Ideas do not survive on their merits. They survive in buildings, in syllabi, in the habits of people who have been taught them by someone whose face they can see.

So this part is deliberately practical, and it is addressed to the people who can actually do these things: campus ministers, diocesan offices, religious orders, publishers, teachers, and the pastor with four minutes in a car park. Five chapters, in the order the work has to happen — listen, publish the questions, build, measure, and wait.
