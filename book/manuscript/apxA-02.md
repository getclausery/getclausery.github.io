---
kind: appendix
title: The Forty Disputed Questions, II
lede: Questions 21–40, including the strongest objections to this book — 40 being whether the whole project is a mistake.
---

**21. Whether words have power over events?**

*It seems so, since Proverbs says life and death are in the tongue.* Words have power over speakers, hearers, and communities, and over events through the actions they cause. They do not act on events directly. The "name it and claim it" teaching is a category error with a proof text. **Practice:** bless rather than curse, and mean it. (Ch. 13, claim 3.)

**22. Whether affirmations work?**

*It seems so, since repetition forms the mind.* Repetition does form the mind, which is why the tradition has aspirations. What works is not the formula but the attention, and attention to a false sentence forms a false self. Choose a true sentence and address it. **Practice:** one verse, three times a day, out loud. (Ch. 27.)

**23. Whether it is wrong to speak of a desired outcome as though it had happened?**

*It seems so, since faith is "the assurance of things hoped for."* Faith's assurance is about God's promise, not about the outcome of my wish. Speaking a false present tense as true trains the speaker in a small dishonesty, and the fruit of that training is visible. **Practice:** speak in the tense of petition and trust. (Ch. 27.)

**24. Whether the name of Jesus can be used as a technique?**

*It seems so, since the apostles used it and the demons fled.* The name is a person. It is powerful because he is, not because the syllables are. Used as a formula, it becomes an incantation, and the Church's own tradition warns against exactly that. **Practice:** say it to him, not at the problem. (Ch. 27.)

**25. Whether illness is attracted by the sufferer?**

*It seems so, since the mind affects the body and some illness is stress-related.* Some illness is mediated by behaviour and stress; none is caused by a "frequency," and the claim that it is has made the sick the authors of their suffering. It is refuted by John 9, by Job, and by the Cross. **Practice:** never explain someone's suffering to them. (Ch. 11.)

**26. Whether suffering is a lesson?**

*It seems so, since "all things work together for good" and we do learn from pain.* God can and does bring good out of evil, and sometimes the good is a lesson. But "suffering is a lesson" as a general explanation assigns a purpose to the evil and a fault to the sufferer. The promise is about God's capacity, not about suffering's meaning. **Practice:** say "God can bring good from this," never "God did this to teach you."

**27. Whether we choose our trials before birth?**

*It seems so, since "soul contracts" explain seemingly meaningless suffering.* The doctrine has no scriptural warrant, and it makes the sufferer the author of her suffering with a longer timeline. It is also unfalsifiable by construction, since the contract is not remembered. **Practice:** learn to say "I do not know why." (Ch. 29.)

**28. Whether it is wrong to want to be healed?**

*It seems so, since the saints accepted illness and "thy will be done" seems to forbid asking.* It is not wrong; it is commanded. We are to ask for our daily bread and for healing, and to add the clause that makes the asking Christian. The error is not in the asking but in the demand. **Practice:** ask for healing, and mean the clause. (Ch. 23.)

**29. Whether grief can be manifested away?**

*It seems so, since "low vibration" is to be avoided.* Grief is not a failure of alignment; it is the price of love, and the Lord wept at a tomb he was about to open. To suppress grief in the name of frequency is to refuse to be human, and it delays the healing it claims to produce. **Practice:** grieve in the company of the Church, which has a liturgy for it. (Ch. 11.)

**30. Whether the past can be revised?**

*It seems so, since memory changes and people report release.* Memory changes, and release is real — but not by denial. The past is forgiven in confession and healed in prayer, and in neither case is it deleted. A past that can be rewritten cannot be forgiven. **Practice:** confess, and keep the record. (Ch. 29.)

**31. Whether the future can be known?**

*It seems so, since the prophets foretold and the saints have had foreknowledge.* God can reveal what he wills, to whom he wills, and the Church tests such claims severely. The disciplined desire to know the future is divination; the disciplined desire to do God's will is discernment. **Practice:** seek counsel, not prediction. (Ch. 30.)

**32. Whether the dead can be consulted?**

*It seems so, since Saul consulted Samuel and the saints are invoked.* The saints are asked to pray, which is an act of the communion of the living; mediums attempt to summon the dead for information, which is forbidden (CCC 2116) and is a traffic in power rather than an act of love. **Practice:** ask the saints to pray for you; do not interrogate the dead.

**33. Whether 11:11 means anything?**

*It seems so, since it keeps happening when I need it.* God can use anything, and gratitude for a coincidence is harmless. But the practice of decoding trains a person to relate to reality as a code and to live in low-grade anxiety, and the Church names it among the omens she rejects. **Practice:** thank God for the moment, and go on with your day. (Ch. 30.)

**34. Whether a Catholic may practise manifestation?**

*It seems so, since much of what it recommends is harmless and some is good.* A Catholic may journall, set goals, imagine, be grateful, and ask God for what she wants. She may not attribute the results to resemblance-causation or treat a technique as efficacious. The verdict is therefore not on the practices but on the framework, and it is a qualified no. (Ch. 13.)

**35. Whether manifestation is a form of divination?**

*It seems so, since it seeks to obtain outcomes by a hidden means.* Divination seeks knowledge or power over the future through hidden powers; manifestation seeks outcomes through a claimed law of the mind. In its "law" form it is superstition rather than divination (CCC 2111); in its "channeling" and "reality-shifting" forms it moves into divination. The distinction matters for the pastoral response. (Ch. 32.)

**36. Whether a priest should refuse absolution to a manifesting penitent?**

*It seems so, since superstition is a sin against religion.* Absolution is refused for lack of disposition, not for confusion. A penitent who is confused, or who uses the language without the metaphysics, should be taught, not refused. Refusal is for the person who persists in a gravely sinful practice with full knowledge and full consent. **Practice:** teach in the confessional, and be slow to refuse.

**37. Whether a Catholic may sell manifestation coaching?**

*It seems so, since the culture sells everything and the market is there.* Selling spiritual power — a technique with a promised effect — is the sin of simony's cousin and fails the Church's own criterion about profit (1978 norms, I.B.c). A Catholic may sell books, courses, and labour; she may not sell the promise of a spiritual result as though it were a product. **Practice:** give it away. (Ch. 35.)

**38. Whether the saints can be "manifested"?**

*It seems so, since people report a saint "coming to them" after a novena.* The saints can intercede and can be experienced as present, and the Church's tradition is full of such accounts, which she tests. They cannot be summoned, managed, or attracted by technique; they are persons, and persons are not mechanisms. **Practice:** ask, and accept that the answer may be no.

**39. Whether the Church has its own manifestation practices?**

*It seems so, since the novena, the Rosary, and the sacramentals look very like them.* She has them in abundance, and this is the book's central practical claim: the manifest space is a parody of a set of practices that the Church possesses and has largely stopped teaching. The answer is not invention but retrieval. (Ch. 26–31.)

**40. Whether this whole project is a mistake?**

*It seems so: because it lends a Christian vocabulary to a system that denies creation; because it invites the Church to spend a generation on a passing fashion; because the author has no mandate; because the resources would be better spent on the poor; and because a parody is best left to die.*

The objection is the strongest in the book, and it is answered only by the criterion that governs every other question here: **does it make people holy?** If the practices of Part Four produce prayer, conversion, charity, and freedom, then the project is justified by its fruit, whatever becomes of its author. If they do not, then the objector is right, and this book should be put down — firmly, and without ceremony — and the work done again by someone else.

That is the risk of any work of this kind, and it is not removed by writing a chapter about it. It is only made honest.
