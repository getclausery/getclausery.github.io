---
kind: appendix
title: Glossary
lede: The technical terms used in this book, in plain language, for the reader who is not a theologian — and for the theologian who wants to know which sense is meant.
---

**Analogy of being (*analogia entis*).** The doctrine that words said of God and of creatures are neither univocal (the same meaning) nor equivocal (no relation), but analogous: the same order of meaning, different mode. The instrument that dissolves "energy," "alignment," and "frequency." (Ch. 9)

**Aspiration.** A short prayer, often scriptural, repeated through the day. The Catholic counterpart of the affirmation. (Ch. 27)

**Charism.** A grace-gift given by the Spirit for the common good (1 Cor 12:7), not an achievement and not under the recipient's control. (Ch. 17)

**Consolation / desolation.** In Ignatian discernment, an interior movement that increases faith, hope, and love and brings peace, or its opposite: darkness, disquiet, and a pull away from God. Not the same as feeling good or bad. (Ch. 30)

**Divinization (*theosis*).** The doctrine that the Christian becomes God by grace, by adoption and participation — not by nature. The true doctrine that the manifest space's "you are God" parodies. (Ch. 13, claim 10)

**Ex opere operato.** "By the work worked": the sacraments confer grace by Christ's action and promise, not by the minister's worthiness. The opposite of a technique, and easily confused with one. (Ch. 20)

**Habitus.** A stable disposition, formed by acts, by which a power is disposed well or ill; the technical term behind "virtue," and the true version of the "law of assumption." (Ch. 24)

**Impetration.** The asking by which we obtain from God what he has ordained to give through asking (ST II-II, q. 83, a. 2). Real causality, not manipulation. (Ch. 22)

**Indifference (*indiferencia*).** Ignatian freedom from inordinate attachment to any outcome, such that one is ready to receive health or sickness, riches or poverty, provided it serves God. Not apathy, and not a technique. (Ch. 23)

**Manifestation (*phanerōsis*, *apokalypsis*).** In Scripture, the making-visible of God in Christ, of the Spirit in the Church, of the children of God at the end. Never a technique of the self. (Ch. 16)

**Manifestation of conscience (*manifestatio conscientiae*).** The disclosure of one's interior to a director or confessor in order to be judged, healed, and directed. The reverse of projection. (Ch. 21)

**Nihil obstat.** In the 2024 norms, the judgment that "nothing stands in the way" of a devotion — without any claim about its supernatural origin. The most useful verdict in the Church's vocabulary, and the one most often forgotten. (Ch. 7, 32)

**Pelagianism.** The teaching that human beings can fulfil God's commands by their own powers without grace. The manifest space is its most complete modern form. (Ch. 10)

**Providence.** The order of all things to their end in the divine mind, executed through real secondary causes, including free creatures. Not fatalism; not deism. (Ch. 22)

**Secondary cause.** A creature acting as a real cause within God's governance. The doctrine that makes human action serious without making it divine. (Ch. 22)

**Superstition.** In the *Catechism*, attributing a magical importance to practices otherwise lawful, or attributing the efficacy of prayer or sacrament to the external performance alone (CCC 2111). The error of the Catholic as well as the manifester. (Ch. 20)

**Tempting God (*tentare Deum*).** Requiring a specified result as the price of faith; putting God to the test. The theological name for the technique that guarantees an outcome. (Ch. 13, claim 14)

**Theodicy of participation.** The claim that the answer to suffering is not an explanation but a presence, a promise, and a transformation. Contrasted with every theodicy that consoles by assigning blame. (Ch. 11)
