---
title: Why It Works
lede: The system succeeds because it meets four real needs badly, in a culture that stopped meeting them well. An answer that does not meet them better will lose, regardless of its truth.
---

There is a way of writing about the manifest space that is satisfying and useless: to explain, at leisure, why it cannot work. The explanation is easy to construct. Thoughts are not a physical force. Like does not attract like. The universe has no preferences. The mechanism is a category error dressed in quantum vocabulary.

All of that is true, and none of it explains why the practice spreads, why intelligent people defend it, and why a person who has been told it is nonsense will go home and write in the notebook anyway.

The honest answer is that the manifest space works — in the only sense that matters to the person using it. It does something. It does not do what it claims, but it does something, and the something is not trivial. This chapter tries to say what.

::scene

**First, it restores agency in a condition of real powerlessness.**

Consider the material conditions of the people most drawn to it. Rents that consume half of a salary. Entry-level work that requires a degree and offers no security. A housing market that has closed. Fertility that has become a medical project. Climate anxiety. A pandemic that demonstrated, to everyone under thirty, that the adults in charge were not in charge.

In that condition, the ordinary levers of agency are not merely difficult; they are largely imaginary. You cannot affect the interest rate. You cannot affect the hiring committee. You cannot affect the diagnosis. And a culture that has no doctrine of providence has nothing to say to a person in that position except "try harder," which is insulting, or "accept it," which is unbearable.

The manifest space says something else: *there is a lever, and it is inside you, and it is available now.* The claim is false. The experience of having a lever is not false, and it is the experience that keeps the practice alive. A young woman who spends twenty minutes a day writing a future she believes she is choosing is not deluded about the interest rate. She is refusing to be a passenger. The refusal is right. The lever is wrong.

**Second, it gives the interior life a practice.**

Traditional Catholic piety at its best is extraordinarily rich in practice: the examen, the Rosary, lectio divina, novenas, fasting, the liturgical year, the veneration of saints, the Stations, the Angelus, the aspirations, the examination of conscience, the direction of a soul. At its worst — and the worst is common — it is a set of obligations with no method, offered to people who have never been taught to pray and who are told, in effect, to figure it out.

The manifest space is all method. It tells you what to do at 11:11, how many times, with what feeling, in what posture, with what words. It has a morning routine and an evening routine. It takes the most nebulous human activity — hoping — and gives it a shape. This is not a small gift, and its absence in the lives of most Catholics is a scandal of catechesis. We have been out-practiced.

**Third, it takes desire seriously.**

There is a strain of Catholic piety that treats desire as a problem to be managed. It is not the whole tradition; it is a deformation of it, but it is common enough that a generation has absorbed it: wanting things is slightly shameful, asking for them is slightly worse, and the mature Christian is the one who has stopped wanting. This is not the Gospel. The Gospel is full of people who ask for things and are not rebuked for asking. "What do you want me to do for you?" is a question Jesus asks twice in the same Gospel.

The manifest space asks the question and waits for the answer. It takes seriously the possibility that a person's desires are information — about what she loves, what she lacks, what she is for. The information is unreliable, as we shall see, and the discernment of desire is a whole chapter of the spiritual life. But the first move, the move of *asking the question at all*, is a move the Church should have made first and didn't.

**Fourth, it is beautiful, and free, and immediate.**

This is not a small matter. The manifest space has excellent design: soft light, quiet voice, a notebook, a candle, an aesthetic of calm competence. It is available at 2 a.m., in bed, for nothing. It asks nothing of you, requires no membership, imposes no schedule beyond the one you choose, and never makes you feel like an outsider at a meeting where everyone else knows the responses.

Compare: a parish young-adult group that meets on a Tuesday when you work, run by a volunteer with three other jobs, in a room with fluorescent lights; a Mass in a language you half-know, at a time you cannot make; a catechesis that ended at fourteen. The comparison is embarrassing, and it is the actual competitive situation.

::scene

None of this makes the system true. It makes it *intelligible*, which is a different and more useful thing. And it lets us say precisely what the Church is up against. The manifest space is not winning because it has better arguments. It is winning because it has better **answers to four questions**, and the Church has been answering different questions:

| The question actually being asked | The manifest space's answer | The answer too often given instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can I do anything at all? | Yes: change your interior | Offer it up |
| How do I pray? | Here is a method, at 11:11, for nine days | Just talk to God |
| What do I actually want? | Write it down, in the present tense | Wanting is dangerous |
| Where can I belong, for free, tonight? | Here, on your phone | Tuesday, in the hall, if you can make it |

The right-hand column is not the Church's teaching. It is the Church's *default pastoral practice* in a lot of places, which is a different thing, and which can be changed.

::scene

Now the wounds. If the manifest space meets four needs, it also inflicts three, and the three are severe enough to make the whole system a net harm — which is the book's judgment, and which should be argued for rather than assumed.

**The wound of blame.** This has been said already and will be said again, because it is the centre of the moral case. A system of interior causation has no innocent suffering. The suffering person is therefore either a failure or a lesson, and in neither case is she permitted to be simply a person in pain who is loved by God. The pastoral consequence is exact: the manifest space cannot accompany the dying. It can only recruit them.

**The wound of dependence on technique.** A practice that promises results produces a particular kind of anxiety: the anxiety of the operator. *Did I do it right? Was my feeling real enough? Did I doubt?* Anyone who has spent time in the space has met this anxiety, and it is structurally identical to scrupulosity — which is a well-known spiritual affliction with a well-known cure, and the cure is not more technique. The manifest space manufactures the disease it claims to heal.

**The wound of the vanished other.** This is the deepest and the least noticed. If everyone is you pushed out, then the person in front of you is not a person. She is a mirror, a messenger, an echo of your assumptions. You cannot love such a being, because love requires an other who can refuse you. You can only manage her. And a world of managed mirrors is a world in which loneliness is not a problem to be solved but a metaphysical condition to be accepted. The manifest space's answer to loneliness is to deny that there is anyone there.

::scene

There is one more thing to say in this chapter, and it is not comfortable for the Church.

The manifest space did not appear in a vacuum. It appeared in a culture that the Church had largely stopped catechizing, at the exact moment when the practices that had formed Catholics for centuries — the rhythms of the liturgical year, family prayer, the examination of conscience, the regular reception of the sacraments — were dissolving. A generation grew up with the vocabulary of the faith and none of its grammar. When they went looking for a grammar, they found one, and it was not ours.

That is not a failure of the young. It is a failure of transmission, and it is the failure that *Fides et Ratio* warned about in another register: a culture that loses its philosophical and catechetical formation does not become neutral. It becomes available. Something will fill it, and whatever fills it will be better adapted to the medium than the thing that was lost.

The rest of this book is about filling it with something true. But the first part of the program is not intellectual at all. It is the recovery of practice — the ordinary, daily, unglamorous formation of Catholics in the grammar of prayer — because a grammar is the only thing that can answer a grammar.
