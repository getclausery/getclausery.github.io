title: Start Here — Three Ways Through This Book

Forty chapters, six parts, eight appendices. You do not have to read it in order, and most people will not. Here is the map, and here are the three routes through it.

::scene

**The map.**

| Part | Chapters | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **One — The Crisis and the Precedent** | 1–7 | Describes the manifest space from the inside, then recovers the method by which Aquinas took Aristotle apart and rebuilt him — including what it cost him |
| **Two — The Audit** | 8–15 | Four tests, fourteen verdicts, a lexicon of thirty words, and an argument for why refutation alone never works |
| **Three — The Reclamation** | 16–24 | The positive theology: manifestation as a divine act, as gift, as glory, as hiddenness, as sacrament; providence, desire, and *habitus* |
| **Four — The Practice** | 25–32 | Six marks, four sets of transpositions, the Novena of Desire, and the triage sheet |
| **Five — The Programme** | 33–37 | What would have to be built: listening, the disputed questions, institutions, measurement, and a fifty-year horizon |
| **Six — Contested Questions** | 38–39 | The strongest objections from the tradition and from the manifest space, answered as far as they can be |
| **Conclusion** | 40 | The girl at 11:11, and the thing the whole world is waiting for |

::scene

**Route one: straight through.** Seven hours or so. The parts are built to be read in sequence, and the argument accumulates: Part Two assumes the method established in Part One, and Part Four assumes the theology established in Part Three. If you have the time, this is the route the book was written for.

**Route two: the short path.** Three hours, eight chapters: **1** (the phenomenon), **3** (why it works), **13** (the fourteen claims and their verdicts), **16** (the word), **22** (providence), **26** (the six marks), **31** (the novena), **32** (the triage), then **40**. Each stands on its own feet. This is the route for a reader who wants the argument without the scholarship.

**Route three: triage only.** Forty minutes: **chapter 32** and **Appendix B**. That is the sheet you photocopy and keep in your breviary or your glove compartment. Come back for the rest when someone asks you a second question — because they will, and the second question is always harder than the first.

::scene

**For the pastor, catechist, teacher, or spiritual director**, who needs something usable by Friday: read Part Two (chapters 8–15) and Part Four (chapters 25–32), then Appendix B. Come back for Part One when you want to know why this is not simply a matter of telling people to stop.

**For the reader who is yourself drawn to manifestation** — if you have a journal, if you have tried the methods, if some of it has worked and some of it has frightened you — begin with Part Three. It is written most directly to you, and it argues that the thing you have been reaching for is real, and larger and stranger and better than the mechanism you were given. Then read Part Two, which will be uncomfortable in places, and which is meant to be.

**For the theologian, scholar, or sceptic**, the argument is in Part One and Part Five. Part One establishes the method by analogy with the thirteenth century and states the four asymmetries that limit the analogy. Part Five sets out the programme, its governance, and the criteria by which it should be judged a failure. Appendix A contains forty disputed questions in outline, which is where I would expect the real argument to happen.

::scene

**For a study group**, eight sessions of ninety minutes will cover the book without rushing it. The pattern that works is: everyone reads the two or three chapters beforehand; the session opens with one question from that week's list; the group argues for fifty minutes; the last twenty are spent on the practice, which is to say on the exercise at the end of the relevant chapter, done rather than discussed.

| Session | Read | Question to open with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | chs. 1–2 | Why do people who have left the Church still believe that their thoughts create reality? |
| 2 | chs. 3–7 | What did Aquinas actually refuse, and what did it cost him? |
| 3 | chs. 8–12 | Apply the four tests to an ordinary piece of spiritual advice you have been given. |
| 4 | chs. 13–15 | Which of the fourteen verdicts do you most want to argue with, and why? |
| 5 | chs. 16–21 | What does the Church already have that the manifest space has counterfeited? |
| 6 | chs. 22–24 | If God is provident, what is left for us to do? |
| 7 | chs. 25–32 | Pray the Novena of Desire as a group, or take the triage and use it on a real case. |
| 8 | chs. 33–40 | What would this parish have to build, and who would pay for it? |

::scene

**A note on order.** Each chapter opens with a promise — the line under its title tells you what the chapter will settle — and closes with three short blocks: **the point** (what it has established), **do this** (something to do, usually in ten minutes), and **what this sets up** (the next question). If you are reading for argument rather than practice, the three blocks will let you skip the exercises without losing the thread; if you are reading for practice, they are the part to keep.

**A note on repetition.** Because most readers arrive through a single chapter that someone sent them, the load-bearing distinctions are repeated where they are needed. This is deliberate. A book that assumes you read page one becomes unusable at page forty, and the person who needs chapter 32 is often the person who will never read chapters 1 to 31.

**A note on tone.** The book is written in the confidence that the reader is intelligent, has a life, and does not need to be scolded. Where it is sharp, it is sharp about ideas and institutions. Where it is gentle, it is gentle about the people who have been hurt by both.

::scene

**The appendices** are not an afterthought; three of them are meant to be used.

**A — The Forty Questions.** The disputed questions in outline, with the state of the argument on each.

**B — The Triage Card.** One page. Photocopy it.

**C — The Thirty-Day Pilot.** A parish or campus programme, with a control group and a written protocol.

**D — The Reader's Guide and Bibliography.** What to read next, in what order, and which ten works are essential.

**E — A Glossary of Thirty Words.** The vocabulary, including the words the manifest space has quietly redefined.

**F — A Note on the Text.** Sources, conventions, and the limits of the author's competence.

**G — Index of Citations.** Every scriptural and catechetical reference, by book and paragraph.

**H — Index of Names and Terms.** Where each figure and idea is treated.
