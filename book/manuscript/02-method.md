title: A Note on Method, Sources, and Authority

**On the object.** "The manifestation space" is not a term of art and there is no magisterial document that uses it. I use it to name a field rather than a school: the loose network of books, videos, coaching programmes, journals, apps, hashtags and private practices gathered around a single proposition — that interior states (thoughts, feelings, assumptions, words, "vibrations") causally produce exterior circumstances, and that the resemblance between the state and the circumstance is the mechanism. The field includes theistic versions (Word of Faith), mentalist versions (Neville Goddard and his many revivals), energetic versions (the Abraham-Hicks material), and thoroughly secularized versions (the self-help wing that speaks of "limiting beliefs" and "self-concept" and leaves the metaphysics vague). It includes practices from *The Secret* to "lucky girl syndrome," from vision boards to "reality shifting," from the 369 method to AI-generated affirmation audio.

**On the term itself.** There is a second, older, Catholic sense of *manifestatio* — the manifestation of conscience to a spiritual director, the epiphany of Christ, the discernment of claimed private revelations. I have not confused the two, but I have made the second do serious work in Part Three, because it turns out to be the strongest available answer to the first. The convergence is not a pun. It is the argument.

**On description before judgment.** Chapter by chapter, I have tried to state the manifest space's positions in the strongest form I could find, from its own texts, before evaluating them. Where I quote Neville Goddard, I quote what he actually wrote. Where I characterize the practice of ordinary adherents, I am relying on the composite portraits described below, on the published sociological and journalistic literature, and on the pastoral experience reported to me by priests and campus ministers. Where I am uncertain, I say so.

**On composite portraits.** The people in this book are composites. Their details have been changed, combined, and in some cases invented, in the manner of reportage; no composite corresponds to a single identifiable person, and no quotation attributed to an anonymous person is a verbatim record. Direct quotations from published authors are real and are cited. I have not invented statistics. Where a number appears, it is drawn from published sources; where I am reasoning from impressions, I say "in my reading" or "the pattern I have seen."

**On citations.** This is a work of synthesis rather than of archival scholarship, and the notes are correspondingly modest: citations appear in the text, in parentheses, in a short form that a reader can follow without a glossary. Primary sources are given by book, question, and article (for Aquinas by part, question, article: *ST* I, q. 1, a. 8). Magisterial documents are given by paragraph number. Appendix D lists the works I have relied on and indicates which are essential.

**On the use of Thomas.** I am not a medievalist and this is not a work of historical theology. The account of Aquinas in Part One is deliberately compressed and is aimed at extracting a *method* rather than settling disputed questions of interpretation. Readers who know the period will find omissions; I hope they will not find falsehoods. The claim I need from the history is narrow and, I think, secure: that the integration of Aristotle was achieved by describing the rival system accurately, separating its instruments from its metaphysics, subordinating its conclusions to revealed principles, rejecting specific theses by name, and institutionalizing the result over decades — and that this process was contested, slow, and at one point formally condemned.

**On authority.** This book has none. It is not a magisterial act, it is not approved by any bishop, and it does not ask the reader to accept anything on its own say-so. Where it proposes practices, it proposes them for supervised experiment. Where it makes doctrinal judgments, it tries to show its work from Scripture, the *Catechism*, and the recognized doctors, so that a competent authority can correct it quickly. A reader who finds an error should assume the error is mine and not the Church's, and should say so.

**On disagreement.** The book argues against positions, not against people. If you practice manifestation and you are reading this, the book is not about how stupid you are. It is about how much of what you have already noticed is true, and what would happen if it were placed where it belongs.
