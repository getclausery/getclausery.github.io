---
title: The Cost of Integration
lede: Three case studies in what happens to the person who tries this — Thomas, Faustina, and the ordinary catechist — and the rules that follow for anyone attempting the same work.
---

Every integration has a bill, and the person who presents it is rarely the one who ordered the work.

This chapter is about the bill. It is placed early, before the audit and before the theology, for a reason: anyone who reads this book and decides to attempt something like it should know what they are purchasing. The pattern is remarkably consistent across eight centuries, and it has three parts.

::scene

**Case one: Thomas, 1277.**

The facts are in the previous chapter and can be compressed. A man spends twenty-five years describing, correcting, and rebuilding a rival system; he dies; two years later a bishop condemns a list of propositions that includes some of his; his order defends him; he is canonized forty-nine years after his death; his condemnation is partly revoked; and six centuries later his method becomes the Church's official philosophy — at which point it stops being a method and becomes a monument, and has to be rescued from its admirers.

Notice the shape. The condemnation came not from the enemy but from the guardians. It came not during his life, when he could answer, but after his death, when he could not. It was aimed at something real (necessitarian philosophy claiming autonomy) and it hit something adjacent (his own carefully guarded positions). And it was reversed by the same institution that had issued it, on a timescale that no individual could have survived.

The lesson is not "the Church is oppressive." The lesson is that institutions protect themselves with categories, and a genuinely new synthesis does not fit the existing categories, and therefore looks, from inside the institution, like the thing it is trying to answer.

**Case two: Faustina, 1959.**

In 1938 a Polish nun died, leaving a diary of claimed revelations and a devotion to Divine Mercy: an image, a chaplet, a feast, and a set of promises. The devotion spread, especially in Poland and among Polish émigrés. In 1959 the Holy Office issued a notification forbidding the spread of the devotion "in the forms proposed by Sister Faustina," on the basis of Italian translations that made parts of the diary read as doctrinally alarming, and of exaggerated promises being propagated with it.

The ban lasted nineteen years. During that time the devotion was not destroyed; it went underground, sustained by the Marians and by ordinary people, and its cause was promoted by the Archbishop of Kraków, Karol Wojtyła, who opened a process in 1965 while the works were still censored in Rome. In 1978 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith lifted the prohibition, having examined better texts. Wojtyła became pope that year. Faustina was beatified in 1993 and canonized in 2000, and the Second Sunday of Easter was given the title Divine Mercy Sunday for the whole Church.

The lesson here is sharper than the first, because the 1959 decision was not made by enemies of the faith and was not correcting a real doctrinal error. It was made, in good faith, on bad information, by an institution with a legitimate duty of vigilance, and it silenced a genuine devotion for two decades. **The guardians can be wrong in the direction of caution, and the cost of that error is borne by the faithful.** Anyone who plans to exercise vigilance over the manifest space should have this case in front of them, because it is the case that shows what vigilance looks like when it is mistaken.

**Case three: the catechist, now.**

The third case is not famous and happens every week. A parish catechist, or a campus minister, or a youth leader, decides to take the question seriously. She reads the sources. She builds a session in which she takes the manifest space's claims apart and offers Catholic practice as the alternative. She is careful, and she is fair, and she is not a heresy-hunter.

Three things happen.

Some of the young people are relieved. They had been carrying an intolerable burden — the belief that their fear had caused their mother's illness — and they had never heard it named. This is the part that is never reported.

Some of the parents are alarmed. A parent who practises manifestation, or who has paid for a coach, hears that the parish is teaching against it, and complains to the pastor. The complaint is not usually about doctrine. It is about the implication that the parent has been doing something wrong.

And some colleague tells her that she is "polarizing," that the Church should be "accompanied rather than judged," and that perhaps this is not the moment. Which is true — it is never the moment — and which has the effect of ensuring that the work is done by people with fewer institutional constraints and less accountability.

::scene

The three cases give three rules, and they are the rules this book tries to follow.

**Rule one: stay inside.** Thomas did not found a movement. He remained a Dominican, submitted his work to the order and the Church, and let them judge it. The integration that survives is the integration that can be corrected. The moment the integrator becomes the founder of a school that cannot be corrected, he has become the problem.

**Rule two: publish the method, not just the conclusion.** Thomas's *Summa* shows its work to an almost absurd degree: objections, authorities, arguments, replies. This is not a stylistic quirk. It is what makes the work corrigible. A conclusion can only be accepted or rejected; a method can be tested. And a method that can be tested is a method that can be improved by people who do not like the author.

**Rule three: do not monetize it.** The Church's own norms for the discernment of claimed revelations list, among the negative criteria, "evidence of a search for profit or gain" (1978 norms, I.B.c). The manifest space is a market. A Catholic response that is also a market will be indistinguishable from it at the level that matters, which is the level of the person deciding whether to trust you. Whatever this work becomes, it should be free at the point of use.

::scene

### The four asymmetries

The analogy with 1277 is the spine of this book, and it has limits. Four of them matter enough to state plainly, because a reader who has spotted them and found them unaddressed is right to distrust everything that follows.

**First: Aristotle arrived as a philosophy; this arrives as a practice.** The Stagirite came in as a corpus — a body of texts with a school behind it, argued over in Latin by men who had taken vows of obedience. You can dispute with a text. You cannot dispute with a habit that someone performs at eleven at night because it is the only thing that has ever made her feel less alone. The consequence is a change of method: the argument has to be carried by a *practice*, not by a refutation, which is why Part Four exists and why it is the longest part of the book.

**Second: Aristotle's system had no theodicy of personal misfortune, and this one does.** The Physics and the Metaphysics do not tell a grieving woman that her grief is her own fault. The manifest space does, constantly, in the gentlest possible voice, and that changes the timetable. A doctrine can wait fifty years for a correct formulation. A sentence that is telling a dying woman she caused her cancer cannot wait a week.

**Third: Thomas had institutions with jurisdiction, and we have platforms without memory.** The *studia*, the chairs, the disputations, the order, the bishop — these were instruments that could decide something and make it stick. The contemporary equivalent is a feed that forgets, a publisher with no doctrinal responsibility, and a diocese that has never heard of the question. We do not get to assume the institutional machinery will do the work, because the machinery is exactly what is missing.

**Fourth, and most important: the rival in this case is derived from us.** Aristotle was a stranger; New Thought is a descendant. It speaks our vocabulary — word, spirit, abundance, thanksgiving, manifestation — with the meanings quietly inverted, and it borrows the shape of our liturgy while denying the God it is addressed to. That makes it far easier to sell to Christians and far harder to distinguish from the faith once it is inside. The discernment required is therefore finer than anything the thirteenth century needed, and the risk is not that the Church will reject the practice. It is that the Church will absorb it without noticing what it has absorbed.

None of these asymmetries destroys the analogy. Each of them changes what the analogy licenses. The method transfers; the timetable does not; the opposition is not a stranger but a cousin; and the instruments have to be built rather than borrowed.

::scene

There are two errors to avoid, and they look like opposites but they come from the same place.

**The error of the ban** treats the phenomenon as a contamination. It is the error of 1210 and of 1959. It assumes that the way to protect the faithful is to remove the dangerous object, and it forgets that the dangerous object is meeting a need that will then be met in some less visible and less corrigible way. When you ban the practice, you do not remove the desire. You remove your own ability to shape it.

**The error of credulity** treats the phenomenon as a dialect. It is the error of the well-meaning synthesis that keeps the metaphysics and adds a crucifix. It assumes that because the practice is moving and the people are sincere, the framework must be basically sound. It forgets that a system can be internally coherent, psychologically effective, and spiritually ruinous, and that the ruin is usually invisible because it happens slowly, in the way that a person slowly stops expecting anything from God.

Between these two errors there is a narrow path, and the Church has a procedure for walking it. It was issued in 1978, revised in 2024, and it is worth describing here, because it is one of the most useful and least known instruments the Church possesses.

::scene

The 1978 norms for judging claimed apparitions and revelations set out a procedure with three stages and two sets of criteria.

The **positive criteria** included: moral certitude about the facts; the personal qualities of the recipients (psychological balance, honesty, moral rectitude, docility to authority); the soundness of the doctrine; and "healthy devotion and abundant and constant spiritual fruit (for example, spirit of prayer, conversion, testimonies of charity)."

The **negative criteria** included: manifest error about the facts; doctrinal error; "evidence of a search for profit or gain"; grave immorality; and psychological disorder.

And the procedure had three steps: first, judge the facts; second, if the judgment is favourable, permit some public devotion with the formula *pro nunc nihil obstare* — "for now, nothing stands in the way"; third, after time and experience, and especially "with special regard to the fecundity of spiritual fruit," express a judgment about authenticity.

The 2024 revision kept the logic and made it more supple, replacing the binary verdict with a graded scale of six possible conclusions: *nihil obstat* (nothing stands in the way, without any judgment about supernatural character); *prae oculis habeatur* (keep it before your eyes — there are elements to note); *curatur* (it needs to be healed or corrected); *sub mandato* (entrust it to a designated authority); *prohibetur et obstruatur* (forbid and block it); and *declaratio de non supernaturalitate* (declare that it is not supernatural).

Read that list again and notice what it is. It is a **graded taxonomy for the pastoral handling of a live spiritual phenomenon**, running from "leave it alone, it is bearing fruit" to "this is a fabrication." It is precisely what the manifest space needs and what the current Catholic conversation lacks, because the current conversation has only two settings: *it's fine* and *it's demonic*.

The taxonomy is translatable. Applied to manifestation practices, it might look like this:

| Verdict | Applied to a manifestation practice |
| --- | --- |
| *Nihil obstat* | gratitude journalling, goal-setting, self-examination, ordinary petition |
| *Prae oculis habeatur* | visualization of a desired good, affirmations about one's own capacities, "manifesting" as colloquial hope |
| *Curatur* | practices with sound instincts and unsound framing — most "Catholic manifestation" content |
| *Sub mandato* | group practices, retreats, anything with a guide and a fee |
| *Prohibetur et obstruatur* | channeling, mediumship, attempts to control others' wills, "reality shifting," curses |
| *Declaratio de non supernaturalitate* | specific claims to guaranteed results, verified failure, fraud |

This is not a solution to the theological problem. It is a way of not making the theological problem worse while it is being solved. It is the difference between a triage nurse and a surgeon, and the Church needs both.

::scene

The last cost is the one nobody warns about, and it is the reason this chapter ends where it does.

If you do this work seriously — if you read the sources, learn the vocabulary, sit with the people, and try to build something true — you will find that you have become strange to both sides. The people in the manifest space will hear the critique in your voice even when you are being generous, because they have heard it before from people who were not generous. And the people in your own community will hear the sympathy in your voice and wonder whose side you are on.

That is the ordinary condition of the integrator, and it is not a sign that something has gone wrong. Thomas was suspected by the Augustinians and by the Averroists, and by the bishop, and by the Franciscans, and by some of his own brethren. He kept working, kept submitting, kept praying, and died before the verdict came in. The verdict, when it came, was not the one he would have written, and it took six hundred years, and it was right in the end.

The work is not to be understood by your own generation. The work is to be correct enough that a later generation can use it, and humble enough that it can be corrected, and free enough that nobody has to pay to hear it.
