---
title: The Cross and the Glory
lede: This is the test the manifest space cannot pass, and cannot be repaired to pass: the test of the innocent sufferer. Everything else in the audit could be argued about. This one is a verdict.
---

There is a room in the basement of the manifest space and I have described it twice already in this book, and I am going to describe it a third time, because it is the reason the book exists.

The room has a bed in it. There is a person in the bed, and the person is dying, and someone who loves her is sitting beside the bed with a book that says that illness is a message from your subconscious, that the body keeps the score, that disease is unexpressed emotion, that you do not get what you want but what you are.

The person in the chair has been reading. She has been trying, in the way that people try when they are desperate, to find the thing she did. She has gone back through the years looking for the resentment, the fear, the moment of low vibration that opened the door. She has apologized to her sister for things she did not do. She has begun to be afraid of her own thoughts, because her thoughts are the only thing in the room that might still be causing this.

The manifest space has no way out of that room. It can only furnish it.

::scene

This is the chapter where the audit stops being an intellectual exercise, so it is worth stating the thesis plainly and then defending it.

**The manifest space cannot account for innocent suffering, and the failure is not an accident of its popularizers but a structural property of its metaphysics. Any version that repairs the failure has stopped being the manifest space.**

The argument is short. If interior states cause exterior circumstances by resemblance, then every circumstance has an interior cause. If every circumstance has an interior cause, then every suffering has an interior cause in the sufferer. If every suffering has an interior cause in the sufferer, then there is no innocent suffering. And if there is no innocent suffering, then the Gospel is not merely incomplete but false, because the Gospel is the story of a man who was innocent and suffered, and who said in advance that this would happen to his friends.

Every softening move the system makes is a variant of the same sentence. "Soul contract" — you chose this before you were born. "Lesson" — you attracted it in order to learn. "Mirror" — the universe is showing you your beliefs. "Growth" — the pain is the resistance leaving. "Shadow work" — the illness is the unintegrated material. Each of them sounds gentler than *you did this*, and each of them means *you did this*. The difference between them is bedside manner.

::scene

Now the alternative, and it is not a theory that explains suffering. That is important. The Church does not have an explanation of suffering, and every attempt to supply one has been, sooner or later, condemned — by the Church herself, because the explanations always end up blaming someone.

What the Church has is not an explanation but a set of sentences and a presence.

**The first sentence is from John 9.** The disciples see a man blind from birth and ask the question that every religious person asks in the presence of suffering: "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" It is a manifest-space question. It assumes that suffering has a cause in someone's interior state, and it offers two candidates. Jesus refuses both: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3).

Notice what the answer does and does not do. It does not say that the blindness is good. It does not say that the man chose it. It does not explain the mechanism. It says that the situation is not a verdict about the man, and that God is going to be present in it. That is the entire Catholic theodicy in one verse: *the sufferer is innocent, and God is not absent.*

**The second is from Job.** Job's friends are the manifest space. They have a system — suffering is proportional to sin — and they spend thirty-five chapters explaining to a suffering man why his suffering is his fault, in increasingly gentle tones. Job refuses. He demands an answer, and he is not rebuked for demanding one. When God finally speaks out of the whirlwind, he does not give an answer. He gives a tour: where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? The speech is not an explanation; it is an exposure to a greatness that makes the demand for an explanation look like what it is — the request of a creature to be shown the workings of a wisdom it could not survive understanding.

And at the end, God says something extraordinary about the friends: "My wrath is kindled against you… for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7). The ones who defended the system were wrong. The one who screamed at God was right. **Every theodicy that consoles by assigning blame is condemned by the book that is supposed to be about the problem.**

**The third is the Cross.** Not the Cross as a transaction — that is another theology, and a good one, but it is not what the Gospels put in front of the sufferer. What they put in front of the sufferer is a man who prayed to be spared and was not. "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). That prayer contains both halves of the Catholic answer and neither half of the manifest one. It asks for what it wants, without embarrassment. It accepts that the answer may be no, without pretending that the no is a yes. And it is prayed by God.

There is no way to make that prayer compatible with the law of attraction. The law of attraction says that a petition that is properly aligned is always granted. The Gospel says that the one perfectly aligned petition in history was refused, and that the refusal was the salvation of the world.

::scene

From these three, a shape emerges, and it is worth naming because it is the thing the manifest space cannot offer.

The Catholic account of suffering is a **theodicy of participation**, not a theodicy of explanation. It does not tell you why. It tells you who is with you, and what your suffering can become, and — this is the part that is hardest to say without sounding glib — that the transformation is real, and that it is not the same as relief.

Three consequences follow, and they are the pastoral content of this chapter.

**First: the sufferer is not asked to be the cause of her own healing.** She is asked to consent, to trust, to receive the sacraments, to accept help, to let herself be loved. That is a completely different assignment, and it is one a dying person can actually complete. The manifest space assigns the dying person an impossible task at the worst possible moment; the Church assigns her a possible one.

**Second: the fear of one's own thoughts is lifted.** A person who has been taught that her intrusive thoughts are causally efficacious lives in a private hell that looks, from outside, exactly like scrupulosity. The Catholic answer is not "your thoughts are powerless" — thoughts are not powerless; they form the soul. It is that thoughts are not *creative*, and that the ones that arrive unbidden are not yours in the way that the system says they are. The tradition has a whole vocabulary for this: temptation is not sin; the suggestion is not the consent; the first movement is not the act. A person who learns that vocabulary is set free from a burden she has been carrying in secret, and I have watched it happen, and it is one of the most direct works of mercy available to a catechist.

**Third: the sick are restored to innocence.** This is the whole of it. Every sentence in the Church's repertoire that is worth anything in that room says the same thing in a different register: *you did not do this, and you are not being punished, and God is not disgusted by you, and you may be angry, and you may ask, and you may not understand.*

::scene

Now the glory, because the test is "cross and glory," and a chapter that stopped at the Cross would be as false as the system it is answering.

The resurrection is not the reversal of the Cross in the sense of a happy ending appended to a tragedy. It is the revelation that the Cross — the actual one, with the actual death — is not the last thing about God. The wounds are still there in the risen body. Thomas is invited to touch them. This is not a detail; it is the answer to the problem. The risen Christ does not appear as someone who avoided suffering. He appears as someone who went through it and came out the other side with the wounds still in him, glorified and not erased.

Which means that the Catholic promise to the suffering person is not: *it will be fine.* It is: *this will be taken up.* The technical word is transfiguration, and it is the only promise in the world that neither denies the reality of the wound nor leaves it as the final truth about you.

The manifest space's promise, by contrast, is that the wound was never real — it was a misalignment, a belief, a frequency. That is not a hope. It is an erasure, and it is offered to people who have lost children.

::scene

There is one more thing to be said, and it is the thing that makes this chapter hard to write honestly.

The Church has often failed this test herself. There is a Catholic version of the manifest space's cruelty, and it is the version that tells the sufferer that her suffering is a punishment, or a test she is failing, or the result of insufficient faith, or — the most refined version — something she should not talk about because it would be a bad witness. The prosperity gospel is not a Protestant invention that stayed in its lane; it has Catholic cousins, and they are just as cruel and rather better dressed.

The test is not "Catholic versus New Age." The test is: **does this allow the sufferer to be innocent?** Applied to the manifest space, the answer is no. Applied to a certain kind of Catholic preaching, the answer is also no. The difference is that the Church has a canon by which she can be corrected — Job, John 9, the Cross, the saints who suffered — and the manifest space has only its own premises. That is what a tradition is for. It is not a guarantee that we will get it right. It is a place where getting it wrong can be named.
