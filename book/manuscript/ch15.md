---
title: Why Refutation Fails
lede: The audit is finished, and it changes nothing by itself. This chapter is about the difference between winning an argument and converting a person — and about the only two things that have ever done the second.
---

If you have followed the argument this far, you are in a dangerous condition: you may believe that the case has been made, and that the case being made is the same as the case being won.

It is not. The history of Catholic engagement with popular spirituality is a long record of excellent arguments that changed nothing. The arguments against spiritualism in the 1920s were correct. The arguments against the prosperity gospel in the 1980s were correct. The arguments against *The Secret* in 2007 were correct. And the movements went on, because a movement is not a proposition, and refutation is a technique that works on propositions.

This chapter tries to say why, because a book that ends Part Two without saying it would be a book by someone who has never tried.

::scene

**The first reason is that people do not hold beliefs in the way the word suggests.**

Newman made the distinction between *notional* assent — agreement with a proposition considered abstractly — and *real* assent — the assent of a person whose imagination and affections have been seized by the thing. A notional assent can be refuted. A real assent cannot, because it is not held as an argument; it is held as an experience, and experiences do not respond to syllogisms.

The manifest space is almost entirely a matter of real assent. Its adherents do not believe in the law of attraction in the way they believe that Wellington is the capital of New Zealand. They believe it because they did the practice and something happened — a coincidence, a shift in mood, a job — and because the practice gave them, for the first time in years, a way of being in the world that felt like agency. You cannot refute that with a paragraph about efficient causation.

**The second reason is that the system is unfalsifiable by construction.** Every failure is evidence of insufficient alignment; every success is evidence of the law. A belief system with that structure is immune to the only evidence that could disconfirm it, and it is also immune to the argument that would, because the argument rests on evidence of the same kind.

**The third reason is identity.** For a great many people, the manifest space is not a hobby; it is the community that took them in when the Church did not, the language in which they learned to hope, and the thing that distinguishes them from their parents. To give it up is not to change an opinion; it is to lose a self. Any argument that does not reckon with this will be heard as an attack, and it will be resisted for reasons that have nothing to do with its premises — which is exactly what the person making the argument will then call irrationality, and which is not irrationality at all.

**The fourth reason is that the system's practices work, in a limited and real sense.** Twenty minutes a day of focused attention on a desired future, combined with gratitude and a refusal to rehearse failure, will change a person's behaviour, and changed behaviour changes outcomes. The person who reports that it works is not lying. She is misattributing the mechanism. And misattribution, when the effect is real, is almost impossible to correct by argument, because the argument appears to deny the effect.

**The fifth reason is the credibility of the arguer.** This is the one that hurts. A Church whose members are known to have committed grave abuses, whose institutions have protected themselves, and whose ministers sometimes speak of suffering in ways that suggest they have never suffered, does not begin the argument with credit. The manifest space's claim to be the only spirituality that takes your desire seriously is believed partly because the alternative is believed to be indifferent. That belief is often unjust. It is not baseless.

**The sixth reason is beauty.** The manifest space is beautiful. The Church, in many places, is not — or rather, the Church's beauty is stored in its liturgy and its art and its saints, and is not available on a Tuesday night on a phone. An argument that is true and ugly loses to a falsehood that is beautiful, over the length of a life.

::scene

If refutation does not work, what does?

The honest answer is: two things, and neither of them is an argument.

**The first is a practice.** People change their metaphysics when they change their practice, not the other way around. The person who begins to pray the examen every night for a month will discover, from the inside, that her interior life is being observed by someone; the theology follows the practice, and it follows it slowly, and it does not need to be argued. This is why the Catholic tradition is a tradition of *ascetical* practices rather than a tradition of propositions to be believed in the abstract. It has always known that the way in is through the body and the day.

Which means that the pastoral answer to "is it okay if I manifest?" is, at the level of practice, almost always the same: *here is something to do instead,* and then a concrete, small, beautiful, repeatable thing. The examen, five minutes, tonight. A novena with a real intention. Adoration, twenty minutes, with a question. The Rosary, badly, in the car. It does not matter much which one, so long as it is real, and so long as the person is not left with the void.

**The second is a person.** Nobody is argued into the Church by a book; people are drawn in by someone whose life has a shape they cannot account for. This is not a counsel of anti-intellectualism. It is a description of the ordinary means: the friend who prays, the priest who is patient, the mother whose grief is not despair, the catechist who takes the question seriously instead of flinching. The argument matters, and it matters mostly *after*: it explains, retrospectively, what the person has already begun to live.

There is a name for this in the tradition, and it is *witness*. It is the reason the martyrs converted more people than the apologists, and the reason the Church's intellectual achievements have always followed her saints rather than the reverse.

::scene

There is a further implication, and it is not comfortable for the institution.

If practice and witness are the means, then the reform that matters most is not a reform of documents. It is the reform of the ordinary: better catechesis, better homilies, better availability of confession, better formation of catechists, more beautiful liturgy, and a clergy and laity who are not exhausted. These are unglamorous things, and they are the actual front line. A parish that teaches one young person to pray the examen, well, for a year, has done more against the manifest space than a conference of theologians writing against it.

None of which makes this book useless. A practice without a theology drifts, and a witness without an argument becomes a personality. The argument is necessary; it is simply not sufficient, and the failure to say so has produced a great deal of writing that is correct and inert.

::scene

One last thing, and it is a warning about the reader's own condition.

The most dangerous outcome of an audit like this one is contempt. It is possible to learn the manifest space's grammar, see through its metaphysics, and come away with a sense of superiority that is itself a spiritual disease — the disease of the man in the Gospel who thanked God that he was not like the other man. Contempt is not a theological virtue. It is not even an intellectual one, since it prevents the holder from learning anything further from the people he has decided to look down on.

The test of whether this book has been understood is not whether the reader can now refute a manifestation video. It is whether the reader can sit with the girl at 11:11, take her seriously, refuse to lie to her, and give her something better — and whether he can do it without needing her to be wrong.

Part Two ends here. The audit has done its work: four claims received, three corrected, seven refused, thirty words translated, and one honest admission that none of it is enough.

Which means the burden now shifts. If refutation cannot do the work, something has to be built. And a thing that has to be built needs a foundation, and the foundation is the subject of Part Three: not what the manifest space gets wrong, but what the Church actually has.
