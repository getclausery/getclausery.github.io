---
title: The Method of the Angelic Doctor
lede: Six moves, in order, with the manifest-space translation of each. This is the operating manual for everything that follows.
---

A method is not a mood. It is a sequence of operations that can be repeated by someone else and checked at each stage. What follows is an attempt to state Thomas's method in that form — six moves, in order — and to translate each one into the work that this book is trying to do.

The sequence matters. Move four is useless without move one, and move six is a fantasy without move five. Most Catholic engagements with the manifest space attempt move five first, which is why they fail.

::scene

### Move one: describe the thing in its own terms

Thomas wrote commentaries before he wrote theology. He translated the Greek rather than relying on the anthology. He could state Aristotle's position so accurately that modern historians still use his commentaries as evidence for what Aristotle meant.

The principle behind this is not politeness. It is the old principle *salvare apparentias* — save the appearances. If your account of a phenomenon cannot reproduce the phenomenon, your account is wrong. If your description of manifestation cannot be recognized by a person who manifests, you have described something else, and your refutation is a refutation of your own caricature.

**Translation.** Read Neville Goddard's *Feeling Is the Secret* and *The Power of Awareness* before writing about him. Read *The Secret*, which is a different and cruder system. Read the Abraham-Hicks material if you can stand it, because it is the most metaphysically explicit version and therefore the most useful. Spend time with practitioners, not with their critics. Ask what they do on a Tuesday, not what they believe in the abstract. And when you write, state their position in a form they would accept — the "steel man," in the current idiom.

This is the stage at which most Catholic writing on the subject fails, and it fails in a specific way: it describes a version of manifestation that no serious practitioner holds (thoughts are magic, you can get anything with no effort), refutes it triumphantly, and is then unable to understand why nobody changes their mind.

### Move two: separate the instrument from the metaphysics

Thomas took act and potency, the four causes, and the treatise on habit. He left the eternal cosmos and the unmoved mover who does not love.

The distinction is not always easy to draw, because in a mature system the instrument and the metaphysics are fused. But it can be drawn, and the test is functional: **does this piece of the system do work that survives the removal of the framework?** The analysis of change in terms of act and potency survives the loss of the eternal cosmos. The claim that like attracts like does not survive the loss of a sympathetic cosmos, because the attraction *is* the cosmology.

**Translation.** The manifest space contains instruments that work and a framework that does not.

Instruments that survive: the observation that attention and expectation shape perception and action; the observation that imagined scenarios rehearse and form desire; the observation that gratitude changes the one who is grateful; the observation that words shape the speaker; the observation that a person's self-concept constrains what she will attempt; the observation that habit is formed by repetition and by feeling; the observation that the interior life is not private.

Framework that does not survive: resemblance-causation; the responsive universe; the identification of the self with God; the denial of innocent suffering; the technique-guarantee.

The distinction has a practical test: **strip the method of its metaphysics and see whether the practice still makes sense.** Gratitude journalling without the law of attraction still makes sense — it is a form of examen. Scripting without the law of attraction makes sense as a way of clarifying and presenting a petition, or as a way of rehearsing a resolution. The 369 method without the law of attraction is a timer. What does *not* survive is the claim that the practice compels the outcome, and that claim was never an instrument; it was the framework.

### Move three: subordinate the instrument to revealed principles

*Sacra doctrina* uses philosophy but does not take its starting points from it (ST I, q. 1, a. 5, ad 2). The technical name for the relationship is instrumental causality: the tool is genuinely the tool's own thing, doing its own work, but the shape of the whole is set by the science that uses it.

This is the move that distinguishes integration from syncretism, and it is the one that is easiest to fake. A syncretist also "uses" the borrowed material; the difference is that in syncretism the borrowed framework quietly sets the agenda. You can tell which one you are looking at by asking: *when the two conflict, which one yields?*

**Translation.** A Catholic practice of manifestation must be one in which, when the technique and the faith conflict, the technique loses — not by being suppressed, but by being reconceived. If the novena "works" and the doctrine of providence is adjusted to explain why, you have syncretism. If the doctrine of providence governs what the novena is understood to be doing, you have integration.

The concrete test: **does the practice still make sense if the outcome does not arrive?** A Catholic practice must be intelligible and good even when the petition is refused. If it only makes sense when it succeeds, it is a technique wearing vestments.

### Move four: correct the master from within

Thomas's most elegant move was to use Aristotle's own distinctions to reach conclusions Aristotle did not reach. Act and potency, taken seriously, yield a creator. The analysis of the soul as subsistent form, taken seriously, yields an immortality that Aristotle's own text seems to deny.

This is more than rhetorical jujitsu. It is a claim about how error works: a system's errors usually sit at its edges, in the places where it has not applied its own best insight. Find the insight, apply it ruthlessly, and the error often falls out.

**Translation.** The manifest space has a best insight, and it is this: the interior life is real, and it is causally connected to the exterior life. It does not apply that insight consistently. If the interior life is causally connected to the exterior life, then it must be connected through *something* — through action, through relationship, through the body, through the structures of the world, through time. The manifest space wants the connection to be immediate and magical, because immediacy is the selling point. But immediacy is not required by the insight. The insight is preserved, and in fact deepened, by a mediated account: interior states → habits → actions → relationships → circumstances, all under providence.

The same move works on desire. The manifest space says desire is authoritative. Its own best insight is that desire is *revelatory* — it tells you what you love. But if desire is revelatory, it can be *wrong* about its object, which is exactly what the tradition says: *amor* is the weight of the soul, and the weight can be misplaced. The manifest space's own premise, taken seriously, demands a doctrine of disordered love. It has none, and so it must deny the evidence of every human life.

### Move five: reject by name, not by category

Thomas did not say "Aristotle is a pagan." He said: *the world is not eternal* (ST I, q. 46, a. 2); *the soul is subsistent* (ST I, q. 75); *God knows singulars* (ST I, q. 14, a. 11); *not all things happen by necessity* (ST I, q. 22; q. 116). Each rejection is a specific thesis with an argument attached.

The reason this matters is not taxonomic neatness. It is that a category-level rejection cannot be answered, and therefore cannot persuade. "It's all New Age" is true of some of it and false of much of it, and the person who hears it knows that it is false of her, and concludes that the speaker does not know what he is talking about.

**Translation.** Part Two of this book does this work: fourteen claims, each with a verdict and a reason. The verdicts are not all negative. Four are accepted, three are corrected, seven are rejected. A response that rejected all fourteen would be as useless as one that accepted all fourteen.

### Move six: institutionalize

Thomas's synthesis survived because it was taught. It entered the curriculum of the mendicant *studia*, then the universities, then the seminaries. It had a second generation (his students), a third, a revival in the sixteenth century, and a magisterial endorsement in the nineteenth. The *Summa* is not a bestseller; it is a textbook, and that is why it is still read.

An idea without an institution is a conversation. The manifest space has institutions — publishers, coaches, platforms, courses, algorithms — and they are extraordinarily effective. The Catholic response will need institutions of its own, or it will be a series of articles that everyone agrees with and nobody uses.

**Translation.** Part Five sets out the institutional programme: a centre, a journal, a formation module, a publisher, a community, and the pastoral tools that a parish can actually use on a Tuesday.

::scene

There is a seventh move, and it is not a technique.

Thomas wrote the *Summa* as an act of prayer. The tradition reports that he said, toward the end of his life, that everything he had written seemed to him like straw compared with what had been shown to him — a sentence that is either the most humble thing in medieval literature or the most terrifying, and possibly both. He did not finish. He stopped writing in December 1273 and died three months later.

The lesson is not that scholarship is worthless. It is that the integration of a rival account of reality is not, at bottom, an intellectual operation. It is a work of love, and love requires the purification of the one who loves. A person who has not been converted by the thing he is trying to integrate will produce a synthesis that is technically correct and spiritually dead — and everyone will be able to tell.

Which is why the next chapter is about the cost, and why the last part of this book is about institutions, and why neither of them is the most important part. The most important part is the one that cannot be put in a method: the practitioner has to become the kind of person who can hold the thing without being held by it.
