---
title: The Sacramental Principle
lede: The Church is not less material than the manifest space. It is far more. That is the argument nobody is making, and it is the one that wins.
---

Here is a claim that sounds wrong and is exactly right: **the manifest space is a form of idealism, and the Catholic Church is the materialist option.**

The manifest space says that reality is at bottom mental, that the world is a projection of consciousness, that the lever is interior, and that matter is a surface on which the inner state writes. The Church says that God made matter, that God entered matter, that God gives himself through matter, and that the body will be raised. One of these is a spirituality for people who are tired of the physical world. The other is a religion of the Incarnation.

This is not the argument the Church usually makes. The usual argument is that the manifest space is worldly — too attached to money, to outcomes, to the goods of this life. And it is true that the manifest space is attached to outcomes. But its attachment is not to the world as such. It is attachment to a *result*, which is a very different thing, and which leaves the world itself untouched, uninteresting, and unreal.

::scene

The technical heart of the Catholic position is a phrase that is badly misunderstood: *ex opere operato*.

The Council of Trent defined it against the Reformers: the sacraments confer grace *ex opere operato*, "by the work worked" — by the fact of their being performed — provided the recipient does not place an obstacle. The phrase sounds, to modern ears, like magic. It is the opposite of magic, and the difference is the entire argument of this chapter.

Magic is a technique. It works by the correct performance of the correct formula, regardless of who performs it, and its power is in the formula. *Ex opere operato* also says that the effect does not depend on the minister's worthiness — but for a completely different reason: because the one who acts in the sacrament is Christ. The minister is an instrument. The efficacy is the fidelity of a person who has promised.

Put the two side by side and the structural difference is plain:

| | Magic / technique | Sacrament |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who acts | the operator | Christ |
| Why it works | the formula is correct | the promise is kept |
| What is guaranteed | the result | the gift |
| What the recipient must do | perform correctly | receive without obstacle |
| Failure means | you did it wrong | nothing, or not yet |

The manifest space is on the left. It is not on the left because it uses rituals — the Church uses rituals. It is on the left because its efficacy is located in the correctness of the operator's interior state. Which is why the manifest space is the most anxious spirituality in the modern world: the operator is always the one who might have failed.

::scene

Now the sacraments themselves, read as the Church's answer to the manifest space's seven hungers.

**Baptism** answers the hunger for a new identity. The manifest space says: assume a new self-concept until it hardens. The Church says: you were buried and raised, once, at a font, and the fact does not depend on your feeling. The identity is not assumed. It is given, and it is indelible.

**Confirmation** answers the hunger for power. The manifest space says: raise your vibration. The Church says: receive the Spirit who gives gifts, and the gifts are for the common good, and they are not under your control.

**Eucharist** answers the hunger for abundance. The manifest space says: attract more. The Church says: here is the body and blood of God, given for you, under the appearance of bread and wine — the most abundant thing in the universe, in the least impressive form. And note the form. The manifest space would have made it a spectacle. God made it food.

**Confession** answers the hunger for a revised past. The manifest space says: rewrite the memory. The Church says: the sin is forgiven, the guilt is removed, the fact remains, and you are sent away with a penance and a fresh start. This is the only real revision of the past on offer, and it works because someone with authority says so, not because you feel it.

**Anointing** answers the hunger for healing. The manifest space says: illness is misalignment, so realign. The Church says: this body is anointed, this suffering is united to Christ's, this person is strengthened, and the outcome is entrusted to God, and death is not a failure of faith.

**Orders** answers the hunger for a guaranteed channel. The manifest space says: learn the method. The Church says: here are men set apart whose ministry does not depend on their talents, so that the gift is not for sale.

**Marriage** answers the hunger for a life that is not alone. The manifest space says: everyone is you pushed out. The Church says: here is a person who is not you, who has promised to stay, and whose otherness is the sacrament.

Seven hungers, seven material signs. Not one of them is a technique, and every one of them is physical.

::scene

The same logic governs the sacramentals — a category almost entirely unknown to ordinary Catholics and directly relevant to this book.

A sacramental is a blessing, an object, an action of the Church that disposes a person to grace without conferring it the way a sacrament does: holy water, the sign of the cross, a medal, a rosary, the blessing of a home, a candle, salt, oil, the blessing of fields and engines and animals. They are the Church's everyday material spirituality, and they are exactly the kind of thing the manifest space invents for itself: crystals, charms, salt lamps, oils, "protection" practices.

The difference is not that one is rational and the other superstitious. Both are material practices with spiritual claims. The difference is the *theology of the object*. A sacramental is a creature that has been blessed — it points beyond itself, it is used in faith, and its power, such as it is, is the power of the Church's prayer and God's goodness. A crystal in the manifest space is a *reservoir* — it stores and emits a force, and its efficacy is quasi-physical and independent of any relationship.

And here is the sting: the Church warns about the misuse of her own sacramentals in exactly the terms she uses against the manifest space. "Superstition," says the *Catechism*, "can even affect the worship we offer the true God, e.g., when one attributes an importance in some way magical to certain practices otherwise lawful or necessary. To attribute the efficacy of prayers or of sacramental signs to their mere external performance, apart from the interior dispositions that they demand, is to fall into superstition" (CCC 2111).

So the Catholic who wears a medal to ward off bad luck, and the manifest-space practitioner who carries a crystal to raise her vibration, are committing the same error, and the Church names it first against her own.

::scene

There is a final inversion in this chapter, and it is the one I would put on a poster.

The manifest space promises that the universe responds to your interior state. The Church promises something stranger and better: that God responds to *matter* — that he gives himself in water and oil and bread and wine and human hands, and that the material world is not a screen for the spiritual but its chosen medium.

Which means that the person who has been taught that everything is in her mind has not been taught to be spiritual. She has been taught to be trapped. The Church's answer is not "think differently." It is: *get up, go to a building, touch water, eat bread, let a man put oil on your forehead, and be told by someone with authority that you are forgiven.* Every one of those is a physical act, and every one of them is a refusal of the idealism that is quietly ruining the spiritual life of a generation.

The manifest space offers a universe made of your own interior. The Church offers a world you can touch, in which God has already touched back.
