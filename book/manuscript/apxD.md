## The tradition

**Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*.** The indispensable text, and more accessible than its reputation. For this book: I, qq. 1, 22, 44–46, 103–105; I-II, qq. 1–5, 49–67; II-II, qq. 83, 95–97. *

**Leo XIII, *Aeterni Patris* (1879).** The document that made Thomas the Church's default, and the source of the motto *vetera novis augere et perferre* — to strengthen and complete the old by aid of the new. Read it for the method, and read it critically for what it cost.

**John Paul II, *Fides et Ratio* (1998).** Especially §§49–51 on the Church's non-ownership of any philosophy, and §§71–72 on inculturation. The corrective to a Thomism that has become a monument. *

**Benedict XVI, *Spe Salvi* (2007).** §§32–34 on prayer as the school of hope and the purification of desire are the theological heart of this book's anthropology. *

**Francis, *Gaudete et Exsultate* (2018).** §§47–59 on contemporary Pelagianism; the diagnosis that applies, with the polarity reversed, to the manifest space.

***Catechism of the Catholic Church*.** 302–314 (providence), 460 (divinization), 946–962 (communion of saints), 1996–2005 (grace), 2111–2117 (superstition and divination), 2735–2741 (prayer and its apparent failure). *

**Pontifical Council for Culture and Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue, *Jesus Christ, Bearer of the Water of Life* (2003).** The most precise official description of the phenomenon in any language, and the most neglected. *

**Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, *Norms… on presumed apparitions or revelations* (1978)**, and the Dicastery's *Norms for proceeding in the discernment of alleged supernatural phenomena* (2024). The governance template, and a usable taxonomy. *

## The sources of the method

**M.-D. Chenu, *Nature, Man, and Society in the Twelfth Century*.** The best short account of the crisis that produced the synthesis.

**Étienne Gilson, *The Christian Philosophy of St. Thomas Aquinas*.** Still the clearest map.

**Josef Pieper, *The Four Cardinal Virtues*.** The best modern introduction to *habitus* for a non-specialist. *

**Erich Przywara, *Analogia Entis*.** Difficult, and the source of the argument in chapter 9. *

**Bernard Lonergan, *Method in Theology*.** On conversion and the levels of operation; useful for Part Five.

**John Henry Newman, *An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent*.** Real and notional assent; the reason chapter 15 exists.

## The manifest space, described seriously

**Neville Goddard, *Feeling Is the Secret* (1944) and *The Power of Awareness* (1952).** The most metaphysically explicit version. *

**Rhonda Byrne, *The Secret* (2006).** The popular synthesis, and the version most critics are actually answering.

**Esther and Jerry Hicks, *Ask and It Is Given* (2004).** The energetic variant, and the clearest statement of the "Abraham" material.

**Kate Bowler, *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* (2013).** Indispensable on the Christian genealogy.

**Beryl Satter, *Each Mind a Kingdom* (1999).** The social history of New Thought.

**Tara Isabella Burton, *Strange Rites* (2020) and *Self-Made* (2023).** The contemporary landscape, written with sympathy and without credulity.

**Eva Illouz, *Saving the Modern Soul* (2008).** The therapeutic register, and why it is so compelling.

## Practice

**Ignatius of Loyola, *Spiritual Exercises*.** §§23 (indifference), 47 (composition of place), 121–126 (application of the senses), 169–189 (election), 313–336 (discernment). *

**John of the Cross, *Ascent of Mount Carmel*.** I.13 (the *nada* maxims) and II.11–12 (on not clinging to apprehensions).

**Thérèse of Lisieux, *Story of a Soul*.** The little way, which is the whole answer to the manifest space's scale.

**Jean-Pierre de Caussade, *Abandonment to Divine Providence*.** The doctrine of the present moment, and a cure for the fantasy of control.

**Romano Guardini, *The Spirit of the Liturgy*.** On sacred time; read with chapter 28.

**Alexander Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*.** The sacramental worldview, from the East.

## A note on what is missing

There is no adequate scholarly literature on the current, post-2020, algorithmic form of the manifest space. The ethnography has not been done; the content audits have not been published; the pastoral data do not exist. That is the largest gap this book points at, and it is the reason chapter 33 is a research plan rather than a summary.

## For a reading group: six sessions

A parish group can work through the argument in six evenings of about ninety minutes, or in
six weeks if each session carries a practice. Read the chapters before the session; the
questions are for the ninety minutes, not for the reading.

**Session one — the question in the room (chapters 1–4).** Read chapters 1, 2, and 4.
Ask first: *what did you already believe about this before you opened the book, and where
did that belief come from?* Then: *chapter 4 says attention, words, imagination, and desire
really do shape a life. Which of those four has been true in your own experience — and which
one do you distrust?* Between sessions: keep the journal of chapter 1 for a week, described
plainly, with no attempt to make anything happen.

**Session two — the audit (chapters 8–12, 13).** Read chapters 8, 9, and 13. Ask: *where
does the engine actually fail — at the metaphysics, at the pastoral practice, or at the
promise?* Then, working from Appendix A, each person takes one disputed question and argues
it as the book does: describe the claim, admit what is true in it, name what must change,
state the practice. Between sessions: take one manifest practice you still use and write
the three-part answer to it for yourself.

**Session three — the reclamation (chapters 16–20).** Read chapters 16, 19, and 20. Ask:
*"manifestation" is a divine word before it is a technique — where in Scripture or the
liturgy have you heard it used that way?* Then: *what would change in your week if you
treated attention as a form of stewardship rather than a resource?* Between sessions: one
hour with no screen, offered as prayer, deliberately unproductive.

**Session four — the practice (chapters 25–30).** Read chapters 26 and 27 before the
session; choose one more transposition between you. Ask: *which of these four transpositions
could your parish actually do this year, and who would have to agree to it?* Then: *what is
the difference between a practice that has provenance and a technique that has an author?*
Between sessions: do one transposition for two weeks, and note what the practice asked of
you that the technique did not.

**Session five — the programme (chapters 31–36).** Read chapter 31 and Appendix B, and skim
chapter 33. Pray the Novena of Desire together, or agree to pray it separately and report
back. Ask: *what would make this novena a mechanism instead of a prayer, and how would we
notice?* Then, from chapter 36: *what fruit would count, and who would be honest enough to
say it did not come?* Between sessions: run the triage card past one person who is not in
the group.

**Session six — the contested questions (chapters 38–40).** Read chapters 38 and 40. Ask:
*which objection in chapter 38 is the strongest, and does the book answer it?* Then: *if
this book is wrong, what would we expect to see in five years that we are not seeing now?*
Close by reading chapter 40 aloud, and then — this is the point of the whole exercise — pray
for the people named in the first chapter, by name if you have them.

One rule for the six sessions: no one is asked to describe a failure of faith in front of
the group. The manifest space recruits by shame, and a group that reproduces the recruitment
has already lost the argument.
