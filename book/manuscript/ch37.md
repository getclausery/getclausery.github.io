---
title: The Fifty-Year Horizon
lede: Five phases, five scenarios, and the discipline of expecting to be condemned. What success would actually look like in 2076 — and why it will not look like a movement.
---

Every plan in the Church is either short-term or dishonest. The pastoral problem is immediate; the formation that would solve it takes a generation; and the institution that would carry the formation takes two.

So this chapter is written on a fifty-year horizon, and it is written with the expectation that the reader will not live to see the end of it. That is not pessimism. It is the ordinary timescale of the things that last.

::scene

### The phases

**Years 0–2: listening and description.** The audit of chapter 33. Sixty to eighty conversations, a content survey, a hundred-page description, published and circulated for criticism. No proposals, no campaigns. The output is a map.

**Years 2–5: the disputed questions.** Forty questions, written by several hands, published one a month, argued with in public. The output is a body of work that can be corrected.

**Years 5–10: the first institutions.** A centre, a formation module in three or four seminaries, a free digital library, a pilot in ten parishes with a control group, and the first published results — including the null ones.

**Years 10–25: formation.** The slow penetration of the material into seminary curricula, lay formation, campus ministry, and the ordinary preaching of a few hundred priests. This is the phase in which the work either becomes part of the furniture or dies, and its success is measured in the number of people who can answer the car-park question without having read this book.

**Years 25–50: reception.** A generation of Catholics for whom providence, petition, discernment, and the manifestation of conscience are ordinary. A body of practice that is simply there, the way the Rosary is there. And, at some point in this phase, a magisterial document that treats the whole question as settled — which will be the signal that the work is finished and can be forgotten.

::scene

### The five scenarios

**1. Ignored.** The most likely outcome for any given book, and the least harmful. The material sits in libraries; a few people use it; the phenomenon mutates and outlives it. The cost is that the pastoral problem is met by improvisation for another generation.

**2. Condemned.** The 1277 scenario, and the 1959 scenario, and the ordinary fate of anything that uses the enemy's vocabulary before the guardians can see the difference between use and surrender. What will be condemned is not the theology but the *permission*: some sentence will be read as saying that a Catholic may practise the law of attraction, and it will be on a list within a year. This is survivable, and it was survived before. The rules are in chapter 7: stay inside, publish the method, do not monetize, do not build a personality.

**3. Co-opted by the market.** The most likely outcome in a culture like ours, and the most damaging. "Catholic manifestation" becomes a genre: journals, courses, retreats, certifications, a subscription. The theology is thinned to a mood, the metaphysics survives under the aesthetics, and the whole thing becomes the thing it was supposed to answer. The tell will be a price list and a promise.

**4. Partially received.** The most likely good outcome. Some of it enters the ordinary life of the Church — the examen, the novena, the discernment of spirits, the triage — and some of it is forgotten. The manifest space fades, not because it was refuted but because its practices were replaced. This is what usually happens, and it is enough.

**5. Absorbed.** A synod, a dicastery, a *vademecum*: the material becomes the Church's own, is corrected in the process, and is taught to people who have never heard of this book. This is the best outcome, and it takes the longest, and it requires that the work be given away rather than owned.

::scene

### Expecting the condemnation

If this work is ever noticed, it will be opposed, and the opposition will come from three places.

**From the guardians of orthodoxy**, who will be right about some of the borrowings and wrong about the method, and who will not be able to tell the difference in the first decade. The response is not to argue in public; it is to submit in private, answer questions in writing, and wait.

**From the market**, which will find the whole thing unprofitable, and will say so in the language of realism: nobody wants doctrine, people want results. The response is to keep the results modest and the doctrine free.

**From the academy**, which will find the work insufficiently rigorous and the phenomenon insufficiently serious. The response is to publish the footnotes and to be corrected.

What is not on the list is persecution, and it is worth saying so. Nobody in this scenario is going to be martyred. The worst that happens to the author of a book like this in a wealthy country is that he is ignored, or mildly censured, or invited onto a panel to be condescended to. That is not suffering; it is admin. The people who will actually suffer in this story are the ones the manifest space is already hurting, and they are the reason the work is worth doing.

::scene

### What success looks like in 2076

Not a movement. A movement would be a failure, because a movement is a thing that can be joined, marketed, and dissolved.

Success looks like this: a young woman in a parish in 2076 has a question about her life, and the first thing she does is pray, and the second thing she does is ask someone, and the third thing she does is write it down. She has never heard of the law of attraction, or if she has, it is like hearing about phrenology — a curiosity from her grandmother's time. She knows the psalms. She has a director. She goes to confession. When her mother is dying, she does not wonder what her mother did to cause it. When her own prayer is unanswered, she is angry, and she says so, and she keeps praying.

That is not a triumph. It is the ordinary life of the Church, which is the only thing this book has ever been trying to restore.

::scene

### The eschatological reserve

There is a reason to end this part with a limit rather than a plan.

"I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Cor 3:6). The work of the Church is not a project with a deliverable; it is a planting, and the growth is not ours, and the harvest is not in our lifetime. This is not an excuse for idleness; Paul planted with great energy. It is a correction of the fantasy that the outcome depends on us — the same fantasy, in its ecclesiastical form, that the manifest space holds in its personal form.

Which means the honest posture for anyone who takes this up is the one Thomas had at the end: to do the work as well as possible, to submit it, to hand it on, and to be prepared to discover that it was straw.

The book you are holding will be obsolete within twenty years. The phenomenon will have mutated; the vocabulary will have moved; the practices will need to be rebuilt. That is not a failure. It is what it means for a work to be alive: it is corrigible. The alternative is a monument, and monuments are what happened to Thomas after six hundred years, and it took a pope to rescue him.

So: plant. Water. Publish the failures. Submit to the bishop. Give it away. And leave the growth to the one whose it is.
