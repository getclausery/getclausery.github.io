---
title: Habitus; or, The True Law of Assumption
lede: The manifest space's most famous doctrine — persist in an assumed state until it hardens into fact — is a real doctrine with the wrong metaphysics. Aquinas has the same insight, better, and calls it habitus.
---

If there is a single chapter of this book that a philosopher should read, it is this one, because it is where the manifest space comes closest to being right and where the cost of its being wrong is clearest.

The claim is Neville Goddard's most quoted sentence, and he wrote it in 1952: *"An assumption, though false, if persisted in, will harden into fact"* (*The Power of Awareness*, ch. 4).

Taken as a claim about reality, it is false, and this book has spent fifteen chapters showing why. Taken as a claim about *persons*, it is one of the most important truths in moral psychology, and the tradition has a technical account of it that is far richer than Goddard's. The account is the doctrine of *habitus*, and it is the instrument by which the manifest space's best insight can be saved.

::scene

Begin with Aquinas's definition, which is dry and repays patience. A habit — *habitus* — is a disposition by which a being is disposed well or ill, either in relation to itself or in relation to its operation (ST I-II, q. 49, a. 1). It is a quality, not an action; it is stable, not fleeting; it is formed by acts and it forms acts.

Four features matter here.

**A habitus is stable.** It is not a mood or an episode. It is a *state* — the very word the manifest space uses, and uses correctly. To have a virtue is to be the kind of person from whom certain acts come easily, and the state persists through changes of feeling.

**A habitus is formed by acts.** We become just by doing just things, brave by doing brave things, temperate by doing temperate things. This is not a slogan; it is a mechanism. Repeated acts leave a disposition, and the disposition makes the next act easier, and the easier act makes the disposition stronger.

**A habitus disposes acts.** Once formed, it shapes perception and desire before deliberation: the generous person *notices* the need; the coward *notices* the exits. The habit operates at the level of attention, which is why it is so powerful and so hard to see.

**A habitus perfects a power.** It does not add a new faculty; it makes an existing one work well. The virtue of courage does not give you a new will; it heals the will's relation to fear.

::scene

Now put Goddard beside this.

*"Assume the feeling of the wish fulfilled, persist in it, and it will harden into fact."*

What has he noticed? He has noticed that an interior state, held with feeling and repetition, becomes a durable disposition, and that a durable disposition changes what a person does, how she appears, what she notices, and therefore — very often — what happens to her. That is a real observation. It is the observation that the *habitus* tradition has been making for twenty-three centuries, and the reason his writing has traction is that it is true at that level.

What has he got wrong? Two things, and they are enormous.

**First, he has made the state into a cause of events rather than a cause of acts.** The habit does not reach out and rearrange the world. It reaches *in* and rearranges the person, and the person then acts, and the acts have consequences, and the consequences are ordered by providence. The chain is longer than Goddard's, and every link is real. What he has done is to delete the middle links and attach the state directly to the outcome, which is not a stronger claim but a magical one.

**Second, he has made the state something you *pretend* rather than something you *receive*.** This is the deeper error, and it is the one that produces the exhaustion. For Goddard, the assumed state is a fiction you inhabit until reality catches up — "an assumption, though false." For Aquinas, the fundamental state of the Christian is not an assumption at all. It is a fact: you are a son, you are justified, you are indwelt. The task is not to pretend it until it becomes true. The task is to *live out of* what is already true, which is a completely different psychological operation and produces a completely different person.

::scene

This is where the theological virtues enter, and they are the answer to the whole book.

Aquinas distinguishes acquired virtues, which are formed by our acts, from infused virtues, which are given by grace (ST I-II, q. 62; q. 63, a. 3). Faith, hope, and charity are not produced by practice; they are poured in. They are habitus in the strict sense — stable dispositions that perfect the powers of the soul — but their cause is God, not us.

Which means that the Christian already possesses the thing the manifest space is trying to manufacture. The manifest space says: assume the state of the wish fulfilled until it hardens into fact. The Gospel says: the state has been given, at Baptism, and it is not a wish fulfilled but a sonship conferred, and the work of a life is to let the acts flow from it.

This is what Paul means by "walk by the Spirit" and what the tradition means by "living in grace." It is not a feeling. It is a *state*, in the precise sense: a habitus, infused, stable, and prior to your feelings about it.

The pastoral consequence is enormous, and it is the single best answer to the manifest space's anthropology that I know. **You do not have to become someone. You have to become who you are.** The manifest space's version — become the person who has the thing — is a treadmill, because the state is always counterfeit and always needs maintenance. The Gospel's version is rest, because the state is real and does not depend on your performance.

::scene

There is a modern corroboration worth noting, not because sociology proves theology, but because it shows that the structure is not exotic.

The empirical literature on habit formation says, roughly, what Aquinas said: behaviour is governed less by decisions than by dispositions; dispositions are formed by repetition in a stable context; and the most durable change comes not from willing harder but from changing the environment and the identity in which the behaviour is embedded. The self-help industry has absorbed this and repackaged it — "identity-based habits," "become the kind of person who" — and the packaging is often closer to the truth than the manifest space's version, because it keeps the causality inside the person.

What none of it can supply is the *ground* of the identity. It can tell you to adopt an identity; it cannot tell you which identity is true. That is a question for metaphysics and for revelation, and the answer the Church gives is the one that makes the practice livable: the identity is not adopted, it is received, and it is received from someone who does not change his mind.

::scene

So what does the practice look like?

It looks like a **rule of life** — a small, stable, repeatable set of acts, ordered to the virtues, reviewed and adjusted. Not a vision board. A rule.

It looks like the **examen**, daily, because the examen is the instrument by which a person notices the movements that are forming her, which is the empirical half of the doctrine of *habitus*.

It looks like the **virtues taken one at a time** — patience for a season, then humility, then courage — because habits are formed by focused repetition and not by generalized aspiration.

It looks like the **sacraments**, because the infused virtues are increased by them, and because a habitus that depends on my performance is not a habitus at all but an anxiety with a schedule.

And it looks like the **acceptance of slowness**, which is the hardest part for a generation formed by the manifest space. Habits are formed over months and years. The manifest space promises a result in a week. The Church promises a character in a lifetime, and then a resurrection.

::scene

One last formulation, and it is the thesis of Part Three in a sentence.

The manifest space's law of assumption is true about persons and false about the world. The doctrine of *habitus* is true about both, because it puts the person where the person belongs — as a creature whose interior life really does shape her action, whose action really does shape her world, and whose whole life is held in a providence that does not need to be tricked.

Which means the true law of assumption is not a technique. It is a grace. Assume the state of the son, because the son is what you are; and then, over a lifetime, the assumption will harden into fact — not because the universe rewards persistence, but because grace perfects nature, and the thing that is being perfected is you.
