---
title: Four Tests
lede: An audit needs criteria that are prior to the thing being audited. These four are not invented for the occasion; each is a load-bearing principle of the Catholic tradition, and each can be applied to a practice as easily as to a proposition.
---

Every tradition has its heresy tests, and every tradition's tests are shaped by the heresies it has actually fought. The Church's tests — the ones that recur from the councils through the *Catechism* — are not arbitrary. They are the four places where the tradition has learned, at enormous cost, that it can be bent out of shape.

This chapter states them in a form that can be used on the manifest space. They are: **Creator and creature; nature and grace; cross and glory; Church and self.** Each corresponds to a principle that the Church has defended against a specific kind of collapse, and each can be applied twice — to the system's *claims* and to its *practices*.

::scene

### Test one: Creator and creature

The principle is the *analogia entis* — the analogy of being. God is not a being among beings, not the largest item in the universe, not a force alongside other forces. When we say that God is good, and that a creature is good, we do not mean the same thing in both cases; we mean something related but infinitely different. This is not a piece of metaphysical fastidiousness. It is the difference between worship and technique.

The test asks: **does this account preserve the infinite difference between God and the world, and therefore the gratuity of everything the world receives?**

The manifest space fails this test at its foundation, and it fails in the specific way the tradition has seen before. It makes the divine continuous with the human: the universe is a field, and the self is a center of the field; imagination is God in us; the law is a law of the self's own nature. The result is a system in which there is no *gift*, because there is no giver who is other. What looks like grace in the manifest space is always the return of one's own investment.

The Council of Nicaea faced a version of this in Arius: a Christ who is not God but the highest creature is a Christ who cannot save, because he is on our side of the line. The manifest space faces a version of it in the opposite direction: a self that is not a creature but a creator is a self that cannot receive, because it has nothing above it to receive from.

### Test two: nature and grace

The principle is *gratia non tollit naturam, sed perficit* — grace does not destroy nature but perfects it (ST I, q. 1, a. 8, ad 2). It has two halves, and both are essential. The first half protects nature: the created order is good, human effort is real, the virtues are real, the world is not a prison to be escaped. The second half protects grace: nature cannot complete itself; the thing that saves is not a natural capacity but a gift.

The test asks: **does this account leave nature its real causality, and grace its necessity?**

Most systems fail one half. The manifest space fails the second half spectacularly: it has an enormous doctrine of human agency and no doctrine of grace at all. Everything is nature, and nature is enough. This is Pelagianism, and it is not a mild error, because a Pelagian universe has no forgiveness in it — only performance, and the failure of performance.

But notice: a great deal of Catholic preaching fails the *first* half, and has done for a long time. A piety that treats all human desire as suspect, all worldly effort as vanity, and all suffering as something to be offered rather than healed is not orthodox; it is a different error, and it is one of the reasons the manifest space looks, to a young person, like the only religion that thinks anything can be done. The test must be applied to us as well, or we will have no standing to apply it to them.

### Test three: cross and glory

The principle is that the Cross is not an interruption of the good news but its centre, and that the resurrection does not cancel the Cross but transfigures it. The technical name for the alternative is a theology of glory — a system in which suffering is a problem to be solved rather than a place where God is found.

The test asks: **does this account have a place for innocent suffering?**

Not a place for *explained* suffering. A place for innocent suffering — for the child with leukemia, for the woman who was raped, for the man who lost his faith along with his wife. The test is not whether the system can generate a sentence about suffering. It is whether the sentence allows the sufferer to be innocent.

The manifest space fails this test completely and cannot be repaired, because the failure is not a bug in its theodicy but the theodicy itself. If interior states cause exterior circumstances, then suffering is caused by interior states, and the sufferer is the author. Every softener — "soul contract," "lesson," "growth," "the universe is refining you" — is a euphemism for the same sentence, which is: *you did this*.

Against this, the Church has two sentences that are worth more than the whole system. The first is from the Gospel: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3). The second is from Job, and it is a refusal to answer: God does not tell Job why, and Job is vindicated for demanding an answer and then accepting that there is one he cannot have.

### Test four: Church and self

The principle is that grace is mediated, and that the mediation is communal and sacramental. The Christian does not relate to God as an isolated unit; she is a member of a body, and the body has a structure, a history, a liturgy, and authority. This is not an administrative arrangement. It is a claim about what salvation is: not a private achievement but an incorporation.

The test asks: **is this practice constitutively communal and embodied, or is it solitary and mental?**

The manifest space fails on both counts at once. It is solitary by design — one person, one journal, one phone — and it is mental by metaphysics, since the lever is interior and the world is a projection. The famous phrase "everyone is you pushed out" is not an eccentricity of Neville Goddard's; it is the logical consequence of the system's premises, and it makes the other person into a mirror. A mirror cannot be loved, because it cannot refuse you.

The Church's alternative is not "be more social." It is the sacramental principle: that God gives himself through matter, in a body, in a place, at a time, through hands that have been ordained and water that has been blessed. This is the point at which the Church is more materialist than the manifest space, and the point at which the manifest space is revealed as a form of idealism — a religion for people who have been persuaded that the only real thing is their own interior.

::scene

Now the second use of the tests, which is the one that matters most in practice.

These four are usually applied to *propositions*, and that is why they are so ineffective in pastoral care. Nobody in a parish presents with a proposition. They present with a practice, and the practice has a metaphysics embedded in it that they have never articulated and would deny if you attributed it to them.

So apply the tests to the practice, by asking four questions that a person can actually answer.

**On Creator and creature:** *"When you do this, are you asking someone, or operating something?"*

**On nature and grace:** *"If you do everything right and nothing happens, whose fault is it?"*

**On cross and glory:** *"What would this say about a friend of yours who got sick and died?"*

**On Church and self:** *"If you had to do this in front of another person, and they could say no to you, would it still work?"*

The answers are diagnostic to a degree that surprised me when I began using them. A person can hold a false metaphysics in the abstract and still answer these questions in a Catholic way, in which case what they have is a vocabulary problem, not a faith problem. And a person can recite the Creed and answer these questions in a manifest-space way, in which case the problem is exactly where the clergy do not look.

::scene

One warning about tests in general, and then we can begin the audit proper.

A test is not a weapon. The moment it becomes a way of classifying people rather than claims, it has failed — not morally only, but epistemically, because the person will sense the classification and stop telling you the truth. The tests in this chapter are meant to be used on sentences, in public, where they can be argued with. Used on souls, in private, without invitation, they are an instrument of the very thing this book is trying to cure.

There is a name for a person who applies the four tests to everyone he meets: a heretic-hunter. The tradition has a word for him too, and it is not a compliment.
