title: Church and the Self
lede: "Everyone is you pushed out" is not a curiosity of one writer. It is the logical consequence of the system's premises — and it is the point at which the manifest space abolishes the other person.
---

Of all the manifest space's doctrines, the one that sounds most like a deep spiritual insight and is in fact the most destructive is the one Neville Goddard's readers summarize as *everyone is you pushed out.*

The claim is that the people in your life are not independent agents but reflections of your own assumptions. Their behaviour toward you is determined by what you hold in consciousness about them. If someone treats you badly, it is because you assume, at some level, that you deserve it. Change the assumption, and the person changes — not because you have persuaded them, but because you have altered the state of which they are an outpicturing. Goddard is reported to have put it in a sentence that is either mystical or monstrous depending on your metaphysics: "The world is yourself pushed out."

Read in one mood, this is a counsel of self-examination, and there is a real insight buried in it: our assumptions about other people do shape how we approach them, and how we approach them shapes how they respond. A person who assumes hostility will find it. That much is true, and it is the beginning of wisdom about one's own contribution to conflict.

Read as the system requires, it is something else entirely: **a denial that there is anyone else.**

::scene

The appeal is real, and it should be stated before it is criticized.

If everyone is you pushed out, then you are never actually at the mercy of another person's freedom. The boss who humiliates you is a state of your own consciousness; the parent who abandoned you is an echo of your assumptions; the man who assaulted you is a mirror. The doctrine offers, to people who have been genuinely powerless, an account in which the power was never really elsewhere. It is the same psychological move as the "soul contract," and it is the same reason it is so attractive: it converts victimhood into authorship, which feels like dignity.

And it works as a *technique of detachment*, in the narrow sense. A person who stops needing another's approval becomes less manipulable, and the doctrine can produce that effect. Some of the people who defend it are defending the only thing that got them out of bed.

But now follow the logic, because the logic is inexorable and it leads somewhere no one wants to go.

**If everyone is you pushed out, then the person who harms you has no moral standing of her own.** She is not a subject; she is a symptom. She cannot be forgiven, because forgiveness requires someone to forgive. She cannot be confronted, because confrontation requires someone to confront. She cannot be loved, because love requires someone who could refuse you. She can only be *managed* — which is to say, manipulated at the level of your own interior, which is the exact opposite of the thing the Gospel calls love of neighbour.

**And the doctrine destroys the possibility of testimony.** If the other person is a projection, then the person who told you about the doctrine is also a projection, and so is the doctrine, and so is your belief in it. The system cannot be defended without assuming the very thing it denies. This is the oldest objection to every form of solipsism, and it is fatal here: *the person who taught you that everyone is you pushed out was not you.*

**And it makes the victims of abuse responsible for their abuse.** This is not a hypothetical misuse; it is the doctrine applied consistently. If another's action toward me is the outworking of my assumption, then the assault was mine. The manifest space has produced, in the last decade, an entire literature of people trying to escape this conclusion while retaining the premise, and the literature is a record of intelligent people performing increasingly elaborate contortions to avoid a sentence that follows from their own first principle.

::scene

### The specific person

If you spend an hour in the manifest space online, you will meet the practice the doctrine was built for. It is usually called **SP** — "the specific person." The technique is simple: choose the person you want, whether or not she has chosen you; write the scene in which she has; feel it as already done; persist until the world rearranges. There are communities with acronyms and journals, "SP journeys" with day counts, celebratory posts when the person comes back, and consoling posts about the bridge of incidents when she does not.

This is not a fringe misuse. It is the cleanest test case the system has, because it removes every ambiguity: the desired good is another person's will.

**The metaphysics fails first.** If everyone is you pushed out, then the person you are working on has no interior life to consent with. She has a script, and you are editing it. The practice is not an attempt to persuade someone; it is an attempt to replace her. Whatever else it is, it is not a relationship, because a relationship requires two centres of action and the technique has arranged for there to be one.

**The ethics fails second, and in a way the system cannot repair.** Love wills the good of the other; this wills the other as my good. Love is glad that the beloved is free, because her freedom is what makes her love worth having; the technique treats her freedom as the obstacle to be dissolved. This is why the practice so often ends in the same place: the person returns, and the practitioner discovers that she has won an argument with a mirror. What she wanted was to be chosen by someone who could have chosen otherwise. She has arranged to be chosen by someone who could not.

**The pastoral reality is third, and it is not contemptible.** Most of the people doing this work are grieving. "SP" is, in the overwhelming majority of cases, an ex — and the practice is a way of not accepting a refusal, which is the oldest and hardest human problem there is. The Church has a word for the place where a refusal is accepted and mourned and survived, and it is not a technique. It is a tomb, and an Easter, and a community that will sit with you in between.

So the transposition is narrow, and it has to be said precisely, because a careless version would be cruel. **You may pray for a person, and for her good, and for her conversion. You may not pray for her will** — not because God could not move it, but because to ask him to move it against her freedom is to ask him to do the one thing he has bound himself not to do, which is to save a person by force. The prayer that is permitted is harder and better: *"Make her happy. Make me the kind of man she would choose freely. And if she does not, make me free of her."* That prayer can be answered. The other one, if it were answered, would destroy the thing it was asking for.

::scene

The Catholic alternative is not "other people are real and your assumptions don't matter." Both halves matter. What the tradition offers is a metaphysics in which the other person is *irreducibly other* — created, free, capable of refusing you, and therefore capable of loving you — and in which your own interior life is genuinely causally connected to hers without being the cause *of* her.

The technical name for this is the doctrine of creation, and the practical name for it is the doctrine of the neighbour.

**Because the other is created, she is a gift.** This is the sentence that the manifest space cannot say and that the Gospel says constantly. The neighbour is not an emanation of my consciousness; she is a work of God, given to me from outside, and the whole possibility of love depends on her not being me. "Love your neighbour as yourself" is a command that presupposes two selves. A philosophy in which there is only one self can produce benevolence — I can be kind to my projection — but it cannot produce love, because love is the act of willing the good of another *as other*, and there is no other.

**Because the other is free, she can refuse, and her refusal is a good.** This is the hardest part. The manifest space promises a world in which no one can refuse you, because you control the reflection. The Catholic faith promises a world in which people can refuse you — and in which God himself can refuse you — and it says that this is worth more than a world of compliant mirrors, because a world without refusal is a world without love, and a world without love is a world in which nothing you receive means anything.

**Because the other is a member, her good is bound up with yours.** The Church's doctrine of the Mystical Body is the exact inversion of the mirror: "If one member suffers, all suffer together" (1 Cor 12:26). This is not a metaphor for solidarity; it is a claim about the structure of reality. We are not particles projecting onto one another; we are members of one body, and the body has a head, and the head is not me.

::scene

There is a practice that makes the difference concrete, and it is so ordinary that its metaphysical content is easy to miss. It is the practice of **asking someone else to pray for you.**

Consider what is happening when a Catholic says to a friend, "please pray for my son." She is asking a person — an other, free, distinct, with her own relationship to God — to intercede. She is not aligning energies. She is not managing a projection. She is acknowledging, in the most practical way possible, that there are at least two of us, and that the second one can do something the first one cannot do alone.

And the same structure appears, at its highest, in the communion of saints. The Catholic asks the saints to pray for her, which is either the most absurd doctrine in Christianity or the most exact expression of what it means to be a creature among creatures before God. It is absurd if the other is a projection, because you cannot ask a mirror for help. It is the whole of reality if the other is a person.

The manifest space has no intercession. It cannot have intercession, because it has no others. And that absence is, I think, the single most reliable diagnostic question in this whole audit: *who do you ask, other than yourself?* If the answer is "no one, I just align," the system has already done its work, and no refutation will reach the person, because the person has been taught that no one is there to reach her.

::scene

One more layer, and then the audit moves on to the specific claims.

The manifest space's answer to loneliness is to deny that there is anyone to be lonely for. The Catholic answer is much stranger and much harder: that loneliness is real, that it is a wound in a real communion, that it is the ache of a member separated from a body, and that it will be healed — not by learning that everyone was you all along, but by being incorporated into a communion of persons who are irreducibly not you, and by seeing, at the end, the face of someone who has been looking at you the whole time.

Everything in the manifest space that is beautiful about connection — the synchronicity, the sense that the world is addressed to you, the feeling that strangers are messengers — is true, and is true for a reason the system cannot name. The world is addressed to you. The strangers are messengers. But they are not sent by your assumptions. They are sent by someone, and they are sent as themselves, and the whole drama of salvation consists in learning to receive them as such.
