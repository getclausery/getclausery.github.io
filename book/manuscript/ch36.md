---
title: Measuring Fruit
lede: The Church has criteria for judging a spiritual movement, and they are better than the metrics of the market. This chapter turns them into an instrument.
---

A movement that cannot be evaluated will be evaluated by its marketing. The manifest space evaluates itself by testimonials; the response must do better, or it will be indistinguishable in kind.

The good news is that the Church already has criteria. They are in the 1978 norms for the discernment of presumed revelations, retained in the 2024 revision, and they are remarkably well designed for the purpose.

::scene

### The Church's criteria

The norms list, among the positive criteria:

- **Moral certitude about the facts** — did this actually happen as described?
- **The qualities of the recipient** — psychological balance, honesty, moral rectitude, docility to authority.
- **The soundness of the doctrine** — is what is being taught true?
- **"Healthy devotion and abundant and constant spiritual fruit (for example, spirit of prayer, conversion, testimonies of charity)."**

And among the negative:

- Manifest error about the facts.
- Doctrinal error.
- **Evidence of a search for profit or gain.**
- Grave immorality in the principals.
- Psychological disorder.

Note the shape. Two of the criteria are about *truth*, one is about the *person*, one is about *money*, and one — the most important for our purposes — is about *fruit*: prayer, conversion, charity. The Church's test of a spiritual movement is not whether it produces experiences. It is whether it produces holiness.

::scene

### What to measure

Translate the criteria into instruments that can be used in a parish pilot. Four primary measures and three safety measures.

**Primary**

1. **Spirit of prayer.** Not "does she pray more" in the abstract, but: does she have a daily practice, does she keep it, and does she describe it as a relationship rather than a technique? A three-question instrument, self-reported, plus a one-month follow-up.
2. **Conversion.** Has anything changed in her moral life? Specifically: has she been to confession, reconciled with anyone, changed a behaviour she had not been able to change? Behavioural indicators, not feelings.
3. **Charity.** What has she done for someone else in the last month, concretely? Hours, acts, money. The *Catechism*'s "testimonies of charity" is not a metaphor; it can be counted.
4. **Freedom.** This is the one that matters most and is hardest to measure. Does she feel able to stop the practice? Does she describe her relationship to God as trust or as performance? Does she report less fear, or more?

**Safety**

5. **Scrupulosity.** A validated scale, or a simple set of questions about intrusive thoughts and confession frequency. The manifest space manufactures this; a Catholic practice should reduce it.
6. **Blame of the sick.** Questions about how the person would explain an illness in someone she loves. A practice that increases this has failed regardless of its other results.
7. **Dependence on a guide.** Does she need the programme, the director, or the author to keep praying? The test of a good formation is that it makes itself unnecessary.

::scene

### How to measure without scientism

Two errors to avoid, and they are symmetrical.

**The first is the substitution of metrics for judgement.** A pilot can show that attendance rose and scrupulosity fell and still be a spiritual failure, because the thing that matters — holiness — is not a number. The numbers are diagnostic, not definitive. They tell you where to look; they do not tell you what you are looking at.

**The second is the refusal to measure at all.** "The fruit will show itself" is true and is often an excuse. The manifest space says the same thing about its own results. A tradition confident enough to be tested should be willing to be tested.

The resolution is the one the Church has always used in the discernment of spirits: **the mixed method**. Numbers, plus the judgement of wise people, plus time, plus the test of the cross. A practice that produces measurable growth in prayer and charity, is judged sound by directors who are not invested in it, survives a decade, and helps people suffer well, is a practice that can be recommended. A practice that produces excitement and dependence, is judged sound only by its promoters, and collapses when the results do not arrive, is not.

::scene

### The pilot design

A single parish, thirty participants, twelve weeks.

**Week 0.** Baseline: the four primary measures, the three safety measures, and one open question — "describe your current practice of prayer."

**Weeks 1–4.** The Novena of Desire (chapter 31), with a weekly group meeting of forty-five minutes.

**Weeks 5–8.** The transpositions, one per week: word and image, time and body, memory and desire, signs and guidance. Each with a written instruction sheet.

**Weeks 9–12.** A rule of life: each participant writes a personal rule of three practices, and lives it.

**Week 12.** Repeat the measures, plus a qualitative interview with each participant, plus a follow-up at six months.

**The comparison group.** A second parish that receives the same amount of attention — a weekly meeting, a course on prayer — without the manifest-space content. This is the control that most parish programmes omit, and without it you cannot tell whether the effect was the content, the community, or the attention.

**The null result.** Publish it. If the course makes no difference, that is data, and it is the kind of data that the manifest space never produces.

::scene

### The instrument

A one-page questionnaire, ten items, scored simply. Draft:

1. In the last week, how many days did you pray in a set form (a psalm, the Rosary, the Jesus Prayer, the Hours)?
2. In the last month, have you been to confession? (Y/N, and whether you would have gone otherwise)
3. In the last month, have you done something concrete for someone else that cost you time or money? (describe)
4. Do you feel able to stop your practice for a week without anxiety? (always / sometimes / never)
5. Do intrusive thoughts frighten you because they might "cause" something? (never / sometimes / often)
6. If someone you loved became seriously ill, would you wonder what they had done to attract it? (never / sometimes / often)
7. Do you pray because you want to, or because you are afraid of what happens if you don't? (want / both / afraid)
8. Has your picture of God changed in the last twelve weeks? (describe in one sentence)
9. Do you have someone you can talk to about your prayer? (Y/N, who)
10. What would you say to a friend who asked you whether to manifest? (two sentences)

Items 5, 6, and 7 are the safety measures. Items 1, 3, 4, and 9 are the fruit measures. Item 10 is the test of whether the formation has become portable — the only reliable sign that a person has actually learned something.

::scene

### The theological criterion

There is one measure that cannot go on the instrument and must not be omitted from the judgement: **holiness**.

Holiness is not measurable, and it is discernible. It shows up as a person who is more free, more loving, more patient, more truthful, less impressed by herself, and less afraid of death. It is recognized by the people who live with her, and it is recognized slowly, over years.

The Church's entire method of canonization is built on this: not on a single miracle, but on a life, a reputation, a body of testimony, and a long examination. That is the model for evaluating this work. Not a launch, not a conference, not a bestseller — a life, over time, examined by people with the authority and the independence to say no.

The manifest space will be judged by its testimonials. The response will be judged by its saints. That is not a fair fight either, but it is the one that matters.
