---
title: Objections from the Tradition
lede: Seven objections from within the Church, each of which has real force, and none of which survives the distinction between use and surrender.
---

The most serious objections to a project like this do not come from the manifest space. They come from the tradition, and they come from people who have reason to be suspicious, and they deserve to be answered carefully.

::scene

### 1. "This is syncretism."

**The objection.** You are proposing to baptize a system whose metaphysics denies creation. That is exactly what syncretism is: the retention of a foreign framework under a Christian name.

**The answer.** It would be, if the proposal were to retain the framework. The whole audit in Part Two is designed to do the opposite: seven of the fourteen claims are rejected outright, and the three "correct" verdicts are corrections of causality, not adjustments of vocabulary. What is being received is a set of observations about attention, habit, imagination, and speech that the tradition already holds — and holding them is not syncretism, it is recovery. The test of whether syncretism has occurred is not whether the vocabulary overlaps. It is whether, when the two conflict, the faith yields. In every transposition proposed in Part Four, the faith governs and the practice is reconceived.

That said, the objection names a real danger, and the danger is not theoretical: the "Catholic manifestation" genre already exists, and much of it *is* syncretistic. This book's disagreement with that genre is the subject of chapter 39's fifth objection.

### 2. "Why not simply preach the Gospel?"

**The objection.** The Church does not need a theory about manifestation. She needs to preach Christ crucified. Everything else is a distraction dressed as relevance.

**The answer.** Agreed, and the book is an attempt to do exactly that — the whole of Part Three is an exposition of Scripture, and the practices in Part Four are the Church's own. But "preach the Gospel" is not a strategy; it is a description of the goal. The question is what the preaching is *about* when the person in front of you believes that her thoughts create reality. A sermon on the Incarnation that does not address the fact that the hearer believes she is a small god will be heard as one more option in a market of options.

The Gospel is preached to *these* people, in *this* culture, with *these* misconceptions. That is what the entire missionary tradition has always done — Paul at the Areopagus, the Fathers against the Gnostics, the Jesuits in China. Preaching the Gospel has never meant preaching it in the abstract.

### 3. "You are giving occultism a hearing."

**The objection.** By treating these practices as serious and describing them sympathetically, you legitimize them.

**The answer.** The opposite is more likely. The practices are already legitimate in the eyes of the people who use them, and they will remain so as long as the only Catholic response is a prohibition issued by someone who has not read them. A serious description, with clear verdicts, is more damaging to the system than a denunciation, because it takes the system's own claims and tests them. Denunciation produces sympathy for the denounced; analysis produces doubt.

Where the objection does have force is in the *tone*: a description that is too admiring, or that lingers on the beauty of the thing, can function as an advertisement. That is a real risk in this book, and it is why the audit precedes the reclamation, and why the verdicts are stated so bluntly.

### 4. "The desire for relevance is the disease."

**The objection.** The Church is dying of the attempt to be interesting to the world. Every generation of accommodation has produced a weaker Church. Stop trying to speak the language of the age and speak the language of the saints.

**The answer.** There is a real and well-documented pattern here, and it is a warning that any project of this kind should take seriously. But it is a warning against *accommodation*, not against *translation*, and the difference is the same as the difference between the two thirteenth-century options: the Averroists accommodated, and Aquinas translated. Accommodation changes the doctrine to fit the audience. Translation changes the vocabulary to carry the doctrine.

The test is simple and severe: does the work make the faith easier to *believe* or easier to *practice*? If the former, it is accommodation and should be stopped. If the latter, it may be the ordinary work of catechesis.

### 5. "The Fathers would have condemned this."

**The objection.** The patristic tradition is ferocious about magic, divination, and theurgy. Read the penitentials, read Tertullian, read Augustine on the astrologers. There is no room for a "Catholic law of attraction."

**The answer.** The Fathers condemned magic, and they were right, and this book condemns it in the same terms (CCC 2111–2117; the fourteen verdicts). What the Fathers also did, and what is less often noticed, is *substitute*: they replaced the pagan practice with a Christian one, at the same time and often in the same place. The feast replaced the festival; the relic replaced the charm; the blessing replaced the incantation; the saint replaced the local spirit. The penitentials are full of substitutions, and the substitution is the operative pastoral strategy.

So the patristic precedent supports the method of this book, not the objection to it. What the Fathers would have condemned is a technique that promises a result and charges a fee. What they would have recognized is the replacement of one practice by a better one, in the same hungry place.

### 6. "You have no mandate."

**The objection.** You are not a bishop, not a theologian with a chair, not a canonized saint. Why should anyone listen?

**The answer.** No reason at all, except that the argument can be tested. This is why the book publishes its method, its sources, its verdicts, and its failures; why it repeatedly tells the reader to submit it to an authority; and why its most ambitious proposal is that the work be taken out of the author's hands. A layman writing about the faith is not a novelty in the Church; it is the normal condition of most of her intellectual life. What would be illegitimate is a claim to authority, and none is made.

### 7. "The Church has its own problems. Fix those first."

**The objection.** You are devoting a book to a popular spirituality while the Church is in crisis — abuse, division, collapsing practice, credibility in ruins. This is a distraction from the real work.

**The answer.** The objection is right about the priority, and wrong about the exclusivity.

It is right that no program of this kind will be credible while the Church's own house is disordered, and that the reform of the Church is the first work. A people who have been betrayed by their shepherds will not be persuaded by a treatise on providence. That is not a reason to write the treatise; it is a reason to be honest about its limits.

It is wrong that the two are alternatives. The person who has been hurt by the Church and the person who has been hurt by the manifest space are often the same person, and what she needs is not a choice between two systems but a community that is actually what it claims to be. The best argument against the law of attraction is a parish that prays and tells the truth. Everything in this book is a description of what that parish would teach.

::scene

There is a pattern in these seven objections, and it is worth naming, because it applies far beyond this book.

Each of them is *true in its warning and false in its conclusion*. Syncretism is a real danger; therefore do not attempt the work. Relevance-seeking is a real disease; therefore do not translate. The Fathers condemned magic; therefore do not distinguish. The Church is in crisis; therefore do not think about anything else.

That structure — a true premise, a non-sequitur, and a counsel of inaction — is the ordinary form of the argument that keeps an institution from doing the thing it most needs to do. The answer to it is not to deny the premise. It is to accept the warning, build the safeguards, and do the work anyway, in the open, where it can be corrected.
