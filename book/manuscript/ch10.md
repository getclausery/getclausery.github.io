---
title: Nature and Grace
lede: The manifest space is Pelagianism with a user manual. The diagnosis is old, precise, and — this is the uncomfortable part — partly true of us.
---

In 2018, Pope Francis published an apostolic exhortation on holiness that contained a chapter on two "subtle enemies" of the Christian life. One was a kind of neo-Gnosticism: a Christianity reduced to an intellectual elite with a private knowledge. The other was a new Pelagianism, and the Pope described it with a precision that reads as though he had been reading the manifest-space literature:

> "Still, some Christians insist on taking another path, that of justification by their own efforts, the worship of the human will and their own abilities. The result is a self-centred and elitist complacency, bereft of true love. This finds expression in a variety of apparently unconnected ways of thinking and acting: an obsession with the law, an absorption with social and political advantages, a punctilious concern for the Church's liturgy, doctrine and prestige, a vanity about the ability to manage practical matters, and an excessive concern with programmes of self-help and personal fulfilment." (*Gaudete et Exsultate* 57)

He was writing about Catholics. He could have been writing about the manifest space, from the other side of the same error.

::scene

Pelagius was a British moralist of the late fourth century who taught that human beings can fulfil God's commands by their own natural powers, without the special assistance of grace. Augustine opposed him for a decade, and the controversy shaped the whole Western theology of grace. The core of the objection was not that Pelagius was optimistic about human nature — optimism about human nature is not a heresy. It was that Pelagius had made grace *superfluous*: if the will can do it, then grace is a decoration, and if grace is a decoration, then the Incarnation is a decoration, and if the Incarnation is a decoration, then the Cross is an example rather than a sacrifice.

Francis's "new Pelagians" are not teaching that the will can fulfil the commands. They are doing something subtler: they are treating the Christian life as a *programme* whose success depends on the quality of one's own execution. The tell is not the doctrine; the tell is the anxiety. A Pelagian is someone who cannot afford to fail, because everything depends on the performance, and who therefore experiences the spiritual life as a series of examinations she is not sure she has passed.

Now read the manifest space through that lens, and the diagnosis becomes exact.

**The manifest space is a total Pelagianism, and a cheerful one.** It has no doctrine of grace at all — not a diminished one, not a disputed one, none. The universe does not give; it responds. The outcome is a function of the practitioner's interior state. Success is alignment, failure is resistance, and the whole moral universe is a meritocracy of vibration. It is the most complete Pelagianism the West has produced since the eighteenth century, and it is sold as liberation.

::scene

The Catholic alternative is not the opposite error, which is a quietism that makes human action irrelevant. It is the doctrine that makes both action and gift possible at once, and the *Catechism* states it with unusual directness:

> "God grants his creatures not only their existence but also the dignity of acting on their own." (CCC 307)

And, on the cooperation of creatures in providence:

> "God is the sovereign master of his plan. But to carry it out he also makes use of his creatures' cooperation. This cooperation is not a passive one; God grants his creatures the dignity of acting on their own and of being causes for one another." (CCC 306)

Read that twice. It says that human action is *real*. It is not a puppet-show, not a decoration, not a mere occasion for divine action. When a woman works for a promotion, her work causes something. When a man forgives his brother, the forgiveness changes the world. The Catholic doctrine of providence is not fatalism; it is the most robust account of human agency on offer, because it grounds agency in the creative act rather than in the creature's own reservoir.

And then it adds the thing the manifest space cannot say: that the whole of this — the agency, the world it acts in, the good it seeks, the power to seek it — is *given*. "What have you that you did not receive?" (1 Cor 4:7).

This is the structure that the manifest space parodies. The parody is exact: it keeps the agency and drops the givenness. And a creature with agency and no givenness is a creature that must manufacture its own worth, which is the definition of hell, and which is why the manifest space, at its most successful, produces people who are exhausted.

::scene

There is a second half to this test, and it is the half that most Catholic writing on the subject skips.

*Gratia non tollit naturam* — grace does not *destroy* nature. The first half of the principle is a charter for the natural. It says that human effort, human skill, human planning, human desire, human medicine, human friendship, and human ambition are not the enemies of grace but its material. It says that a Christian may set a goal, work for it, visualize it if it helps, write it down, tell a friend, and pursue it with everything she has, without any of that being Pelagianism — because Pelagianism is not the presence of effort; it is the absence of dependence.

This distinction is the pastoral key to the whole book, and it is worth stating as bluntly as possible.

**A Catholic may do almost everything the manifest space recommends. What she may not do is believe what the manifest space believes about why it works.**

She may write down her desires, because desire is real and articulable. She may rehearse a difficult conversation in her imagination, because the imagination is a real faculty and rehearsal is a real form of prudence. She may keep a gratitude journal, because thanksgiving is a duty and a medicine. She may speak a hoped-for future aloud, because words shape the speaker. She may work for the promotion with all her strength, because the world is real and effort is a real cause.

What she may not do is attribute the result to the resemblance between her thought and the event, because that attribution is not a harmless piece of folk psychology. It is a claim about who God is — namely, that there isn't one, only a law — and it will eventually exact its price, and the price will be paid by someone who is sick.

::scene

Which brings us to the hardest sentence in this chapter.

The manifest space is not wrong because it thinks too highly of human beings. It is wrong because it thinks too *lowly* of them. It offers a creature a universe made of her own interior, which sounds like a promotion and is actually a demotion: a being who can create reality by assumption is a being with no reality to be given, no gift to receive, no other to love, no death to die into, and no God to adore. In the manifest space, the human person is infinite and alone.

The Catholic account says something that is harder to believe and better to live with: that the person is finite, dependent, limited, and *loved* — loved by someone whose goodness is not a projection, whose gifts are not returns, and whose providence is not a law but a face.

The next chapter takes up the consequence of that, which is the Cross.
