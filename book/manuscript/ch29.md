---
title: Transpositions III: Memory and Desire
lede: "Revision," "shadow work," limiting beliefs, soul contracts — the manifest space's four attempts to deal with the past. The Church's answer is older, harder, and actually works.
---

The past is where the manifest space is most obviously making something up, and where the Church has the most to offer. Four practices, and four substitutions.

::scene

### 1. "Revision" → confession and the healing of memory

**What the manifest space does.** Mentally rewrites a past event — imagining it as it should have been — on the theory that the revision changes the event's effect on the present, or the event itself.

**What is true in it.** The past is not finished with us. An event can be carried in a way that poisons the present, and the way it is carried can genuinely change. A memory can lose its sting. A person can be released from a history. All of this is true, and the manifest space is not wrong that something can be done.

**What must change.** The mechanism, and the honesty. Revision works by denial: it says that the event, in some sense, did not happen as it happened. That is false, and it is also pastorally disastrous, because the person who has denied the event has no way to be forgiven for it and no way to grieve it.

**The practice, part one: confession.** The sacrament does what revision claims to do, and does it by the opposite means. It *keeps* the fact and *removes* the guilt. It names the thing out loud to a witness. It receives a word from outside: *I absolve you.* And it gives the penitent something to do — a penance — which converts the past into a future act of love.

The theology here is worth stating because it is the whole answer to "revision": the past of a forgiven sinner is not deleted; it is *redeemed*. The fact remains, the guilt is gone, and the event is taken up into a story that now ends differently. That is a more radical claim than revision, and it is the only one that can survive contact with the truth.

**The practice, part two: the healing of memory.** Where a memory still wounds after confession — and it often does, because the wound is psychological as well as moral — the tradition offers a prayer that is simple and has a long pedigree. Take the memory, in prayer, and ask Christ to be present in it. Not instead of the event; *in* it. Then, in the imagination, let him look at the person you were, and say what he would say. This is not revision. It is the application of the Incarnation to a memory: the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, and "among us" includes the worst room in your history.

**Caution.** This practice is powerful and is not a substitute for therapy. Where the memory involves trauma, it should be undertaken with a competent director and, where appropriate, a clinician — not alone, and not as an attempt to force a feeling.

::scene

### 2. "Shadow work" → the examination of conscience

**What the manifest space does.** Identifies the disowned, rejected parts of the self and integrates them, so that they stop sabotaging the manifestation.

**What is true in it.** There is a great deal in a person that she does not want to see, and it operates below the level of choice. The tradition calls it the passions, the wounds, the *fomes* — the tinder of sin — and it has been studying it for centuries with more precision than the modern literature.

**What must change.** The goal. Shadow work aims at *integration*: the disowned part is brought into the self and accepted. The Christian practice aims at *conversion and confession*: the thing is brought into the light, named, judged, forgiven, and healed. Integration leaves you in charge of your darkness. Confession puts you under a mercy.

**The practice.** The examination of conscience, done properly, is not a scrupulous audit of faults; it is the Ignatian review of the day's movements. Fifteen minutes, three questions:

1. **What did I feel today, and where did it come from?** (Not "what did I do wrong" — first, what moved me.)
2. **Which of those movements led me toward love, and which away?**
3. **What is the one thing I will do differently tomorrow?**

Once a month, take the pattern to confession. The shadow, named in the presence of a confessor with authority, is a very different thing from the shadow named in a journal at midnight.

::scene

### 3. "Limiting beliefs" → the discernment of spirits

**What the manifest space does.** Treats every sense of limitation as a false belief to be removed, because the only obstacle to the desired outcome is the belief that it is impossible.

**What is true in it.** Many beliefs are false, and they do limit. A person who believes she cannot learn, cannot be loved, or cannot change is being held by a lie, and the lie should be broken.

**What must change.** The assumption that *every* limit is a belief. Some limits are real: finitude, illness, the freedom of other people, the consequences of one's history, death. A practice that treats these as beliefs to be deleted will produce either a fantasy life or a collapse, and it will do so with the cruelty of a system that says the collapse was your fault.

**The practice.** Discernment. For any belief that seems to be holding you back, ask: *Is this a lie, or is it a truth I do not want?* The two are handled completely differently. A lie is renounced and replaced with the truth (which is often a scriptural sentence). A truth is accepted and grieved, and then worked with. The confusion of the two is one of the most common causes of spiritual damage in the manifest space, and it is the reason the Church insists on the discernment of spirits as a discipline rather than a mood.

::scene

### 4. "Soul contracts" → the refusal to explain

**What the manifest space does.** Explains suffering by saying that the soul chose it before birth, for a lesson, in a contract agreed to in another life.

**What is true in it.** The instinct behind it is good: it refuses to accept that suffering is meaningless, and it looks for a purpose beyond the obvious.

**What must change.** Everything. The soul contract is a theodicy that assigns the cause of suffering to the sufferer — which is the same move as "you attracted it," only with a longer timeline and a gentler vocabulary. It is also, pastorally, a closed system: the person cannot argue with a contract she does not remember making.

**The practice.** Learn to say, and to mean, *I do not know why.* This is not a failure of faith. It is the answer of Job, of the psalmists, of the Church in the presence of the innocent sufferer. What the Christian has is not an explanation but a presence, a promise, and a future; and the discipline of not explaining is one of the works of mercy.

When someone offers you a soul contract as a consolation, the kind response is: *I know you mean to help. I don't think the reason is available to me. What would help is if you sat with me.*

::scene

### 5. The journal → the ledger of providence

There is one practice I would recommend above all the others in this chapter, and it is the one nobody teaches.

Keep a written record of your petitions and of what happened.

The manifest space cannot keep such a record, and this is one of its most revealing limitations. Its literature is entirely composed of successes, because its epistemology requires it; the failures are either unrecorded or reclassified as misalignment. This produces the strange effect that a practice used by millions has almost no honest archive.

A Christian can keep an honest archive, because a Christian can survive an unanswered prayer.

**The practice.** A notebook, one page per month. In one column, the date and the petition. In a second, the date and what happened — *granted*, *refused*, *different*, *not yet*, *I no longer want it*. In a third, what changed in me.

Do this for three years and you will possess something that no manifestation journal can contain: a record of providence. You will see patterns you could not have seen in the moment; you will see prayers that were answered in ways you did not recognize; you will see the ones that were refused, and, if you are honest, you will sometimes see why. And you will learn the thing that the whole practice is designed to teach: that you have been dealt with, personally, patiently, over a long time, by someone who was not a mechanism.

::scene

The four substitutions in this chapter have a common shape, and it is the shape of the whole book. The manifest space takes a real human task — dealing with the past, facing the shadow, breaking false limits, finding meaning in suffering — and offers a technique that avoids the one thing that would actually accomplish it: the presence of another.

Confession requires a confessor. Direction requires a director. The healing of memory requires Christ. The refusal to explain requires a community that can sit with the unexplained. Every one of them is a relationship, and every one of them is therefore slower, harder, and real.
