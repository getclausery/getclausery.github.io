---
title: Why Practice Beats Doctrine
lede: Human beings are not formed by what they believe but by what they do repeatedly. The manifest space knows this. That is the real reason it is winning — and the reason the response has to be a practice, not a pamphlet.
---

There is a fact about Catholic life that is embarrassing to state plainly, and this chapter is going to state it.

The Church possesses the most sophisticated, most beautiful, most tested system of spiritual practice in the history of the human race: a daily sacrifice, a cycle of readings that covers the whole of Scripture, seven sacraments, a Liturgy of the Hours that has been prayed without interruption for fifteen centuries, a thousand years of monastic discipline, the Rosary, the examen, *lectio divina*, fasting, pilgrimage, the veneration of relics, the liturgical year with its fasts and feasts, and a library of ascetical theology that no other tradition can approach.

And a generation of Catholics has grown up knowing almost none of it as *practice*, having received it instead as *obligation* — a list of things one must do, with no instruction in how, no explanation of why, and no experience of the effects.

The manifest space arrived in that vacuum with twelve pages of method and took the field.

::scene

This is not a failure of doctrine. It is a failure of formation, and the distinction matters because the remedy is different.

Doctrine is transmitted by teaching. Practice is transmitted by *apprenticeship*: you do it with someone who knows how, badly at first, and you get better. The Church has always known this — it is the entire logic of the liturgy, of the novitiate, of the *Exercises*, of the family rosary — but in the last sixty years the apprenticeship has collapsed in many places while the teaching has continued, so that Catholics can now explain transubstantiation and cannot pray for twenty minutes.

The manifest space did not win by having better doctrine. It won by having an *apprenticeship*: a video shows you exactly what to do, tonight, in ten minutes, with your own hand and your own pen. That is a form of transmission, and it is thousands of years older than the printing press.

::scene

There is a principle behind this, and it is the one this book has already used: *lex orandi, lex credendi* — roughly, the law of prayer establishes the law of belief. What a community does in worship shapes what it can believe. Doctrine is not the opposite of practice; it is the sediment that practice leaves.

This has two consequences, and they cut in opposite directions.

**The first is encouraging.** You do not need to win the argument first. If you can get a person to pray the examen for a month, the doctrine will follow, because the practice will have taught her something that no argument could. She will have discovered, from the inside, that her interior life is observed; that her desires are mixed; that she is capable of being moved by something that is not her; that gratitude changes her. None of that needs to be argued. It needs to be done.

**The second is sobering.** The reverse is also true: a bad practice teaches a bad doctrine, whatever the catechism says. A parish that celebrates the liturgy carelessly teaches, without a single word, that the Eucharist is not the body of Christ. A devotional life that consists of anxious bargaining teaches that God is a mechanism. A catechesis that consists of worksheets teaches that the faith is a school subject. The manifest space does not need to argue with us; it only needs to be more *formative* than we are.

::scene

Which is why this part of the book is not an appendix. It is the heart of the proposal, and if it fails, everything before it was a long way of saying nothing.

The test of the transpositions that follow is not whether they are doctrinally correct. It is whether a person will do them, and whether, having done them for a month, she is more free. If the practice is correct and nobody does it, it has failed. If the practice is done and the person is unchanged, it has also failed, and the doctrine is not to blame; the design is.

So the criteria for a good transposition are severe, and they are worth stating before we look at any of them:

1. **It must be small enough to start tonight.** Five minutes, not an hour. A person in crisis does not have an hour.
2. **It must be repeatable.** Daily or weekly, at a fixed time, with a fixed form, because formation happens through repetition.
3. **It must be embodied.** Posture, place, object, breath. A practice that exists only in the head is a practice that can be abandoned without noticing.
4. **It must be addressed to a person.** Not to a mechanism, not to the self in the abstract. "Lord" or "Father" or "Jesus," said, not thought.
5. **It must be open to refusal.** A practice that promises a result is a technique. A practice that can be answered with "no" is a prayer.
6. **It must be intelligible without a lecture.** The person should be able to grasp the shape of it in a paragraph, even if its depths take years.
7. **It must be given, not sold.** The manifest space is free at the point of use. So must this be.

::scene

There is one more reason to put practice at the centre, and it is the reason that is easiest to miss.

The manifest space's hold on people is not primarily intellectual. It is *experiential*: people have had experiences in the practice — of focus, of hope, of a strange coincidence, of a shift in mood — and those experiences are the anchor. You cannot argue a person out of an experience. You can only offer her a deeper one.

This is why the transpositions in the next four chapters are not "Catholic alternatives" in the sense of consolation prizes. They are attempts to put a person in the way of the experiences that the manifest space counterfeits: the experience of being heard, of being known, of being changed, of being spoken to, of receiving what one did not earn.

Those experiences are real. They are available. They are, in fact, more available than the counterfeit, because they do not depend on the practitioner's performance and they do not stop working when the outcome does not arrive.

The only thing they require is a form of life in which they can happen — and the provision of that form is what a Church is for.
