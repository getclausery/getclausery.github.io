---
title: Transpositions II: Time and Body
lede: Eleven-eleven, the moon phases, the state akin to sleep, the 3 a.m. waking — four temporal and bodily practices with real effects, and the Church's far older discipline of sacred time.
---

The manifest space is unusually attentive to *when* and *how* one practices. There are times (11:11, the new moon, the hour before sleep), postures (lying down, hand on heart), rhythms (morning and night), and bodily techniques (breath, repetition, the state between waking and sleeping). This is not superstition. It is a genuine insight: human beings are temporal and bodily creatures, and a spirituality that ignores time and the body will be a spirituality of the mind only, which is to say, a spirituality that will not survive a bad week.

The Church is, if anything, more attentive to these things than the manifest space. What follows is the transposition.

::scene

### 1. Eleven-eleven and the moon → the liturgical calendar

**What the manifest space does.** Watches for repeating numbers as confirmations, and organizes practice by lunar phases (new moon for intentions, full moon for release).

**What is true in it.** Sacred time is real. Some moments *are* different, and a person who marks time is a person whose life has a shape. The manifest space has discovered this and, having no calendar, has improvised one out of clocks and moons.

**What must change.** The calendar. The Church has a calendar that is two thousand years old and covers the year with fasts, feasts, seasons, saints' days, and the whole life of Christ. It is the most sophisticated Christian answer to the human need for sacred time, and most Catholics use almost none of it.

**The practice.** Begin with three days a year, and let them be real: Ash Wednesday (the fast), Good Friday (the silence), and your baptismal anniversary (the feast). Then add the Sunday obligation as what it is — the first day, the day of resurrection, the day that makes the week. A person who keeps the liturgical year for three cycles will find that time has become a story rather than a series of Tuesdays.

::scene

### 2. The state akin to sleep → Compline

**What the manifest space does.** Uses the hypnagogic state — the borderland between waking and sleeping — for "SATS": the assumption of the wish fulfilled, repeated as one falls asleep, on the theory that the subconscious is most receptive then.

**What is true in it.** The state before sleep is spiritually significant. The last thing on your mind shapes the night, and the night shapes the morning. Every monastic tradition has known this; Compline exists because of it.

**What must change.** The content and the addressee. SATS fills the borderland with a manufactured scene. Compline fills it with the Psalms, the *Nunc dimittis*, the confession, and a blessing — and then the great antiphon of the tradition: *Salva nos, Domine, vigilantes; custodi nos dormientes* — "Save us, Lord, while we are awake; guard us while we sleep."

**The practice.** Night prayer, five minutes, from a breviary app or a printed copy. If you have no book: one psalm (4, 91, or 134), the *Nunc dimittis* ("Lord, now let your servant go in peace"), an examination of the day in three questions (what went well, what went badly, what will I do tomorrow), and the sign of the cross. Then sleep.

**Why it works.** Because it does to the night what SATS tries to do and cannot: it puts the night under someone else's care rather than under the care of your own imagination.

::scene

### 3. The 3 a.m. waking → the night office

**What the manifest space does.** Nothing, mostly. The 3 a.m. waking is a problem for a system that promises peace, and it is usually interpreted as resistance or as a sign that the practice is not working.

**What is true in it.** The night is when a person is alone with her thoughts, and the tradition has an entire literature for this: the night office of the monks, the psalms of lament, the "dark night" of John of the Cross, the insomnia of the saints.

**What must change.** The interpretation. The 3 a.m. waking is not a failure of your frequency. It is one of the ordinary conditions of being a creature, and the tradition has a practice for it: get up, or lie still, and say Psalm 63 — "O God, you are my God, for you I long; for you my soul is thirsting" — or the Jesus Prayer, or the Rosary, badly, in the dark, until you sleep.

The point is not to eliminate the waking. It is to make it a place where you are not alone. There are people who have prayed in that hour for fifteen hundred years, and joining them is a real act of communion, and it is available at 3 a.m. in a way that almost nothing else is.

::scene

### 4. Breath and repetition → the body at prayer

**What the manifest space does.** Uses breath work, tapping, bilateral stimulation, humming, and repetition to regulate the nervous system and "raise the vibration."

**What is true in it.** The body is involved in prayer, breath is a real instrument, and repetition has real effects on attention and on the nervous system. Anyone who has prayed the Rosary badly and felt calmer knows this.

**What must change.** Almost nothing at the level of technique, and everything at the level of meaning. The Church has its own embodied repertoire, and it is older and richer:

- **Kneeling** — the body's confession that it is not in charge.
- **Prostration** — on Good Friday and at ordinations, the most absolute gesture in the tradition.
- **Fasting** — the body's participation in a desire that is not for food.
- **Pilgrimage** — walking as prayer, with a destination that is not metaphorical.
- **Incense, water, oil, candles** — the senses enlisted.
- **Silence** — the hardest and most bodily of all, because it requires an actual body in an actual room doing nothing.

**The practice.** Choose one bodily practice and one time. Kneel for the Our Father. Fast on Fridays, in some real way, and give the money saved to someone. Walk to a church once a week rather than driving. The body will complain, which is the point: the complaint is the practice.

::scene

### 5. The Sabbath → the great refusal

There is one practice in the tradition that has no manifest equivalent at all, and it is the one most needed by a generation formed in the manifest space.

**The Sabbath** — the day on which you do not optimize.

The manifest space has no Sabbath, because everything is potentially an opportunity to align, manifest, improve, or optimize. Its logic is total: every hour can be worked, every mood can be adjusted, every outcome can be influenced. This is why its most successful practitioners are exhausted, and why the aesthetic of the manifest space is a strange combination of calm and strain.

The Sabbath is the structural refusal. One day in seven, you do not work, you do not optimize, you do not manifest. You rest, you worship, you eat with people, you do nothing useful. And you do it not because it is efficient but because you are not a machine, and because the world is held by someone else, and because the refusal to be productive is an act of faith.

**The practice.** Pick a day, or a half-day, and keep it. No work. No self-improvement. No screens if you can. Mass, a meal with people, a walk, a nap, a book. And when the guilt arrives — and it will, because the manifest space has trained you to feel guilty about unproductive time — say the sentence that the Sabbath exists to teach: *the world is not mine to run.*

::scene

The pattern in all five transpositions is the same as in the previous chapter. The manifest space is not wrong that time and the body matter. It is wrong about what they are *for*, and it has improvised a calendar, a night prayer, and a bodily discipline out of almost nothing, while the Church's versions sit unused in the breviary and the calendar.

Which is an indictment, but also an opportunity, and it is the reason this chapter is practical rather than polemical. The material is already there. Somebody has to hand it to people, with instructions, on a Tuesday.
