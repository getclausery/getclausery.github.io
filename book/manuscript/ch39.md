---
title: Objections from the Manifest Space
lede: Seven objections from the other side — including the two that are genuinely hard: that prayer is just manifestation with extra steps, and that the mystics said the same thing.
---

A book that only answers its friends is a pamphlet. The objections from the manifest space are, in several cases, sharper than the objections from the Church, and two of them require real work.

::scene

### 1. "You're limiting God."

**The objection.** You say that we cannot compel outcomes. But Jesus said, "Whatever you ask in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours" (Mark 11:24). You are putting a hedge around a promise that God did not hedge.

**The answer.** The verse is real, and the Church has never explained it away. She has, however, read it in the context of everything else Jesus says about prayer, and that context includes three things that the manifest space omits.

First, the same Jesus prays "not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39) — and he is the only person in history whose prayer was perfectly aligned. Second, James explains why some prayers are not answered: "You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, to spend it on your passions" (James 4:3). Third, the Church's whole doctrine of impetration assumes that the promise is made to prayer *in the name of Jesus*, which means prayer according to his will and his character, not a formula attached to a desire.

So the hedge is not mine. It is in the text, and it is there because the alternative is a God who is not free, which is to say, not God. A promise without a condition would make prayer into a mechanism and God into a function of the petitioner. The Church limits God in exactly one respect: she refuses to make him limited by us.

### 2. "The Church is a controlling institution."

**The objection.** You are asking people to hand their interior lives to a hierarchy that has abused children, silenced victims, and told the sick that their suffering is God's will. Why would anyone trade a practice that belongs to them for an institution that has proved it cannot be trusted?

**The answer.** There is no clever reply to this, and the attempt to give one would be obscene. The abuse is real, the cover-up is real, and the loss of credibility is a just consequence.

What can be said is this: the alternative on offer is not freedom. The manifest space has its own authorities — coaches, influencers, platforms, and an algorithm that decides what a lonely person sees at 2 a.m. — and they are unaccountable in a way that the Church, at its worst, has never quite managed, because the Church at least has a law, a court, a public record, and a long tradition of being criticized from within. The choice is not between an institution and no institution. It is between an institution that can be held to account and a market that cannot.

And the deepest point: the Church's authority is not her own. She claims to forgive sins in Christ's name, not her own; to teach what she has received, not what she has invented. When she acts as though the authority were hers, she becomes exactly what her critics say she is. The remedy is not to abandon the authority but to purify its exercise — which is what reform is, and which is why the reform of the Church and the answer to the manifest space are the same work.

### 3. "Prayer is just manifestation with extra steps."

**The objection.** This is the hard one. Strip away the theology and you have two practices: an interior act of desire, repeated, with an expectation of an external effect. You say the difference is the addressee. But an addressee you cannot see, hear, or verify is functionally indistinguishable from a law with a personality.

**The answer.** The objection is strongest when it is made against bad Catholic preaching, and there is a great deal of bad Catholic preaching that is functionally a law of attraction with a beard: name the promise, claim the verse, expect the result, and if the result does not come, you did not believe enough. The Word of Faith movement is not a Protestant eccentricity; it is the same structure, and it exists in Catholic devotional practice too.

But the difference between prayer and manifestation is not the addressee. It is **the shape of the act**.

A manifestation is an act that treats the outcome as the point. Its success condition is the result, and its failure condition is the absence of the result. A prayer is an act whose point is the relationship, and whose success condition is fidelity — which is why the Church can say that a prayer was heard and refused, and mean both.

You can test which one a person is doing by asking what happens when the result does not come. The technique has no resources; it can only re-describe the failure. The prayer has resources, because the relationship survives the refusal — indeed, the refusal is where the relationship is most itself. That difference is not visible from outside, which is why the objection has force. It is entirely visible from inside, which is why people who have prayed through a refusal do not make this objection.

### 4. "You've never tried it."

**The objection.** You write about manifestation as a critic who has read the books. Anyone who has actually practised it knows that something happens that your categories cannot explain.

**The answer.** Partly true, and it is the reason chapter 4 exists. I have taken the practice seriously enough to say that it works in a limited and real sense: attention, expectation, rehearsal, and gratitude all change a person, and a changed person changes her circumstances. What I deny is the mechanism, and I deny it on the grounds that a better mechanism — habit, action, relationship, providence — explains the same data without the cost.

The cost, moreover, is not hypothetical, and it is not paid by the critic. It is paid by the woman who believes she caused her mother's cancer. If the practice's only defence is that it works for the healthy, it has already lost the argument.

### 5. "The mystics said the same thing."

**The objection.** This is the second hard one. Meister Eckhart, the author of the *Cloud of Unknowing*, the Sufi poets, the Upanishads — they all speak of the identity of the self with the divine, of creation by consciousness, of the world as appearance. Your "orthodox" position is a Latin administrative narrowing of a much wilder tradition.

**The answer.** There are real and striking parallels, and any honest account has to grant them. The tradition of apophatic mysticism does speak of the ground of the soul, of union, of the dissolution of the separate self, of a world that is not what it seems. The manifest space drew on Neoplatonism, and Neoplatonism is in the Church's bloodstream.

Three distinctions, however, do the work.

**First, direction.** The mystic's dissolution of the self is *kenotic*: the self is emptied, and the emptying is a death, and it is accomplished by grace and not by technique. The manifest space's expansion of the self is *imperial*: the self is enlarged until it is coextensive with reality, and the enlargement is accomplished by a method. "I am God" said by a man who has been annihilated by love is a different sentence from "I am God" said by a woman with a journal, even when the words are the same.

**Second, Christology.** The Christian mystics are, without exception, Christological. Eckhart's ground of the soul is not a generic cosmic self; it is the place where the Word is born. The *Cloud* is a treatise on love of God in Christ, not on the divinity of the practitioner. When the manifest space quotes the mystics, it quotes the sentences about union and omits the sentences about the Cross, and the omission is the whole difference.

**Third, verification.** The Church tested her mystics. Eckhart was investigated; some of his propositions were censured. John of the Cross was imprisoned by his own order. The discernment was not a formality: it was the mechanism by which the tradition distinguished genuine union from spiritual inflation. The manifest space has no such mechanism, which is why it cannot tell the difference between a saint and a salesperson — and neither can the person reading at 2 a.m.

### 6. "It works for me."

**The objection.** I am not interested in your metaphysics. I started doing this at the worst time of my life, and my life is better now. Who are you to tell me it is false?

**The answer.** Nobody. And I am not telling you that your life is not better. I am telling you that the thing that made it better may not be the thing you think, and that the difference matters — because the belief that your interior state *causes* events is the same belief that will tell you, on the day something terrible happens, that you caused it.

Keep the practice. Change the explanation. That is not a demand for conversion; it is an invitation to keep the good thing you have found and lose the thing that will hurt you.

### 7. "You are afraid of your own power."

**The objection.** The whole book is a long argument for a smaller self. You are frightened of the possibility that human beings are more than the Church has told them.

**The answer.** The Church's claim about the human person is not small. She says that a human being is made in the image of God, redeemed by the blood of God, indwelt by the Spirit, destined for divinization, and raised in a body that will be like Christ's. She says that the saints will judge angels. It is difficult to imagine a higher account of the human person, and it is the manifest space's account that is finally small: a being whose only power is to rearrange its own projections, in a universe with no one else in it.

The difference is not the size of the self. It is whether the self is a creature. And the creature's power is greater than the creator's, because the creature can receive, and the creator can only invent.

::scene

Two of these objections — that prayer is manifestation with extra steps, and that the mystics said the same thing — cannot be answered by assertion. They can only be answered by a life.

A person who prays through a refusal, and stays; a person whose union with God has made her more ordinary and less impressive; a person who has been emptied and not enlarged — that person answers both objections, and she does it without an argument, and the answer is more convincing than this chapter.

Which is, in the end, the argument of the whole book. The Church does not win this by being right. She wins it by being holy, and holiness is the one thing the manifest space cannot manufacture, because it is the one thing that is given.
