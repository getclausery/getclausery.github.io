What the book does

It takes the manifestation movement as a rival account of reality and does what Thomas Aquinas did with Aristotle in the thirteenth century: it describes it in its own terms, tests it, refuses what is false, keeps what is true, and rebuilds the rest inside the faith.

  • The manifest space's seven rules — and their datable history: Quimby, Evans, Hopkins, Eddy, the Fillmores, Holmes, Goddard, Byrne. Every rule has an author and a year.
  • Aquinas's six moves of reconstruction, with the four limits of the analogy stated rather than hidden.
  • Four tests — who is creator, what is nature, what is the cross, and who is this for.
  • Fourteen central claims, fourteen verdicts: four to integrate, three to correct, seven to reject.
  • A constructive programme: six marks of a Catholic practice, four transpositions of the methods people actually use, a nine-day Novena of Desire, a one-page triage card for the priest in the car park, a thirty-day pilot with controls, and forty disputed questions where the real argument belongs.
If the argument is right, the question is not whether the Church can survive the manifest space. It is whether the Church will do, with a generation formed by it, what she once did with Aristotle.

What it is not

A theological research project, not an authoritative text. No imprimatur has been sought or granted, and none is claimed. It was written with the help of the Holy Spirit and is submitted to the Church for correction — the first is a claim about help received, the second a claim about authority, and only the second is a bishop's to make.
