How to write an independent contractor agreement

Published 17 October 2026 · Freelancing and getting paid

An independent contractor agreement records the deal between a client and a self-employed service provider: the work, the price, the timetable, ownership and how the relationship ends. Write those decisions down before work starts. A clear agreement reduces disputes, but it cannot turn an employee into a contractor simply by calling them one.

1. Identify the parties and check the relationship

Use each party's legal name, business address and contact details. If the contractor operates through a company, name that company rather than only the individual doing the work. Record who may approve deliverables, changes and invoices for the client.

Then check whether contracting is appropriate. Control over how work is performed, independence and the practical working arrangement can matter. The US tax and wage-law sources below explain different tests; other countries have their own. Do not copy a status declaration into an arrangement that actually functions as employment.

2. Define the work and the acceptance process

Describe deliverables rather than saying only “consulting services.” List the format, quantity, milestones, assumptions and exclusions. Identify any client inputs, such as access, product information or approvals, and what happens if they arrive late. For a detailed project, attach a statement of work and give it a version and date.

The Contractor will deliver a five-page website design in the agreed design-file format,
including two consolidated revision rounds. Copywriting, hosting and ongoing maintenance
are excluded. The Client will provide final copy and brand assets by [date]. Additional
work requires written agreement on the scope, fee and revised delivery date.

Set a reasonable review period and a way to report defects against the agreed specification. Distinguish correcting an error from requesting a new feature. The statement-of-work checklist helps make acceptance measurable.

3. Make fees and payment dates unambiguous

Choose a fixed fee, hourly rate or retainer. State the currency, deposit, invoice schedule and due date, and whether taxes and expenses are included. If expenses need approval, set the approval method and any cap. Never leave “payment on completion” undefined when completion depends on somebody answering an email.

For example, a USD 2,400 project might require USD 800 before the start and USD 1,600 after the agreed final milestone, payable within 14 days of the invoice. That is an illustration, not a recommended rate. Use the payment terms clause to consider disputes, overdue sums and lawful late charges.

4. Describe independence without making false promises

A contractor may control their methods and supply their own equipment, subject to agreed results, lawful site rules and necessary security requirements. Say whether subcontracting needs consent and whether the contractor can bind the client to purchases or other commitments. Do not promise benefits or impose employee-style supervision by accident.

The independent contractor clause explains the limits of status wording. A clause allocating tax responsibilities does not remove withholding, reporting or other duties that the law puts on the client. Review the written terms alongside what both sides will actually do.

5. Decide ownership and confidentiality separately

Specify which deliverables the client receives and whether ownership transfers or a licence is granted. State when the transfer takes effect, such as after full payment where lawful. Reserve the contractor's pre-existing tools, methods and reusable material, while giving the client the rights needed to use the finished work.

Third-party fonts, stock photographs and software may have separate licence terms. Identify those limits rather than promising ownership of everything. The intellectual property clause covers the main choices. Confidentiality should define protected information, permitted use, reasonable safeguards and exceptions, including disclosures required by law; it should not become an unlimited ban on the contractor's future work.

6. Agree how the project can end

State the start date, any end date, notice method and termination rights. Separate ending for convenience from ending for a serious breach, and decide whether a breach can be corrected first. Explain what is payable for completed work and approved commitments, and what happens to an unused advance.

Either party may end the engagement on [number] days' written notice. On termination,
the Client will pay for work properly performed up to the end date and approved,
non-cancellable expenses. The Contractor will provide the agreed paid-for work in progress
and return or delete confidential information, subject to lawful record-retention duties.

This is sample wording to adapt, not a complete termination regime. Address access removal, handover and clauses that continue after termination. Read the contract termination guide before sending a notice under an existing agreement.

7. Review risk, signing and the final document

Check warranties, insurance, liability limits, indemnities, governing law and dispute resolution against the actual risk and local law. A small design job should not quietly carry an unlimited guarantee for the client's entire business. Obtain appropriate professional advice for regulated services, sensitive data or substantial potential losses.

  • Make sure every attachment is named, dated and consistent with the main agreement.
  • Remove contradictory terms copied from an employment contract or another project.
  • Confirm that the signers have authority and that any required formalities are met.
  • Keep the signed version, approved changes, invoices and acceptance messages together.

Questions

Does signing a contractor agreement make someone self-employed?

No. Classification depends on the actual relationship and the legal test that applies, not just the contract label. Tax, employment and local rules can use different tests. Get advice if the person will work like an employee.

Can an independent contractor agreement cover several projects?

Yes. Use a master agreement for recurring terms and a signed statement of work for each project, with its own deliverables, dates and fees. Say which document controls if they conflict.

Who owns work made by an independent contractor?

Do not assume that paying the invoice transfers copyright. State what is assigned or licensed, when that happens, and what tools, pre-existing material and third-party rights are excluded. Local law may require particular wording.
